Coherent states and photon statistics

Coherent state in the Fock basis
\[ |\alpha\rangle=e^{-|\alpha|^2/2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}}\,|n\rangle \]
  • \(|n\rangle\)state with exactly n photons
  • \(\bar n=|\alpha|^2\)mean photon number
  • \(|\langle n|\alpha\rangle|^2\)Poisson law with parameter \(\bar n\)

Example: \(\bar n=0.5\) gives \(P(0)=e^{-0.5}=0.6065\), \(P(1)=0.3033\), \(P(\ge 3)=0.01439\). Hence a Fock cut-off at \(d=16\) is enough for small \(\bar n\).

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
A weak laser pulse has no fixed photon number; it behaves like a Poisson bag of photons. A coherent state encodes exactly that.

Non-orthogonality of coherent states

Overlap of two coherent states (Eq. 2)
\[ \langle\alpha|\beta\rangle=\exp\!\Bigl[-\tfrac12\bigl(|\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2\bigr)+\alpha^{*}\beta\Bigr],\qquad |\langle\alpha|\beta\rangle|^2=e^{-|\alpha-\beta|^2} \]
  • \(\alpha^{*}\beta\)conjugated product
  • \(|\alpha-\beta|^2\)squared distance in phase space

Two states are never orthogonal for finite \(\alpha\ne\beta\): \(|\langle\alpha|\beta\rangle|^2>0\), so no single measurement separates them perfectly.

The two BPSK states and their overlap

The BPSK pair \(|\pm\alpha\rangle\) (Sec. II-E, App. E)
\[ \langle\alpha|{-}\alpha\rangle=e^{-2|\alpha|^2},\qquad |\langle\alpha|{-}\alpha\rangle|^2=e^{-4|\alpha|^2}=e^{-4\bar n} \]
  • \(|\psi_0\rangle=|{+}\alpha\rangle\)bit 0
  • \(|\psi_1\rangle=|{-}\alpha\rangle\)bit 1

Short proof (Eqs. 79 to 82): \(\langle\alpha|{-}\alpha\rangle=e^{-|\alpha|^2}\sum_n\frac{(-|\alpha|^2)^n}{n!}=e^{-|\alpha|^2}e^{-|\alpha|^2}\).

RememberThe larger \(\alpha\), the faster \(e^{-4|\alpha|^2}\) falls: \(\bar n=1\) gives \(e^{-4}=0.01832\), almost orthogonal.
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
The two bits are two pulses of equal amplitude and opposite phase. They still slightly "touch", so a minimum error always remains.

The Helstrom bound for two mixed states

General Helstrom bound (Eq. 3, 25)
\[ P_e^{\min}=\tfrac12\Bigl[1-\operatorname{Tr}\bigl|\,p_1\rho_1-p_0\rho_0\bigr|\Bigr]=\tfrac12\Bigl(1-\sum_k|\lambda_k|\Bigr) \]
  • \(p_0,p_1\)prior probabilities
  • \(\lambda_k\)eigenvalues of \(\Delta=p_1\rho_1-p_0\rho_0\)
  • \(|X|=\sqrt{X^\dagger X}\)operator absolute value

The optimal measurement is a two-element POVM: \(Q_1=\sum_{\lambda_k>0}|\phi_k\rangle\langle\phi_k|\), \(Q_0=I-Q_1\) (Sec. VI-A).

Mind the signThe paper writes \(\operatorname{Tr}|p_1\rho_1-p_0\rho_0|\) then replaces it in Eq. (26) by \(\sum_k\lambda_k\) (no absolute value). It must be \(\sum_k|\lambda_k|\); \(\sum_k\lambda_k=\operatorname{Tr}\Delta=p_1-p_0\).
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Form the weighted difference of the two density matrices, take all eigenvalues and add their magnitudes. The larger the sum, the easier the discrimination.

The Helstrom bound of pure BPSK

Helstrom bound for two pure states (Eq. 4, 5)
\[ P_e^{\min}=\tfrac12\Bigl(1-\sqrt{1-c^{2}}\Bigr),\qquad \text{BPSK: } P_e^{\min}=\tfrac12\Bigl(1-\sqrt{1-e^{-4\bar n}}\Bigr)\approx\tfrac14e^{-4\bar n} \]
  • \(c=|\langle\psi_0|\psi_1\rangle|\)overlap
  • \(\bar n=|\alpha|^2\)mean photons

The three-node architecture of the base paper

Each hop is a channel \(\beta_i=\sqrt{I}\,\psi_i+\epsilon\) (fading \(I\), noise \(\epsilon\)). Charlie Helstrom-decodes then re-encodes: \(P_e\approx P_{e,1}(1-P_{e,2})+(1-P_{e,1})P_{e,2}\).

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Replace one long path by two short ones; a station in the middle receives and retransmits (decode-and-forward).

The Gamma-Gamma law of received irradiance

Gamma-Gamma irradiance density (Eq. 6)
\[ f_I(i)=\frac{2(\alpha\beta)^{\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}}}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}\,i^{\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}-1}K_{\alpha-\beta}\!\bigl(2\sqrt{\alpha\beta\,i}\bigr),\quad i>0 \]
  • \(K_\nu\)modified Bessel function, 2nd kind
  • \(\alpha\)large scale (beam wander)
  • \(\beta\)small scale (scintillation)

\(\mathbb E[I]=1\) and the scintillation index is \(\sigma_I^2=\tfrac1\alpha+\tfrac1\beta+\tfrac1{\alpha\beta}\). For \(\alpha=4.0,\beta=1.9\): \(\sigma_I^2=0.9079\).

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Light through the atmosphere flickers. This law gives the probability of each brightness level; its mean is always 1.

Rytov variance versus link length

Rytov variance (Eq. 7, 11)
\[ \sigma_R^2(L)=C\,C_n^2\,k^{7/6}L^{11/6},\quad k=\tfrac{2\pi}{\lambda},\; C=1{,}23 \]
  • \(C_n^2\)index structure
  • \(L\)link length (m)

\(\lambda=1.55\,\mu\)m, \(C_n^2=5\cdot10^{-14}\), \(L=1200\) m give \(\sigma_R^2=1.391\).

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Doubling the link multiplies the turbulence strength by about 3.6 (\(2^{11/6}\)).

Eq. (8) and (9) exactly as printed

Printed form: denominator \(1+1.11\sqrt{\sigma_R^2}\), i.e. \(\sigma_R^{1}\). Andrews–Phillips form: \(1+1.11\,\sigma_R^{12/5}=1+1.11(\sigma_R^2)^{6/5}\). They differ a lot once \(\sigma_R^2>1\).

Confirmed from pages 3 and 4The page images show a square root of \(\sigma_R^2\) (power 1), not \(12/5\). The project code keeps both: standard and legacy_branch_main.
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
These two formulas turn the turbulence strength \(\sigma_R^2\) into two numbers \(\alpha,\beta\). The print uses a square root, the textbook a 6/5 power.

The fading plus noise channel and the mixed state

Received signal and density matrix (Eq. 14 to 16)
\[ \beta_i=\sqrt{I}\,\psi_i+\epsilon,\quad \epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2);\qquad \rho_i(I)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p(\beta)\,|\beta\rangle\langle\beta|\,d\beta \]
  • \(\psi_i=\pm\gamma\)sent amplitude
  • \(\epsilon\)real Gaussian noise
  • \(\rho_i\)mixed state at the receiver

For fixed \(I\), \(\beta_i\sim\mathcal N(\sqrt I\psi_i,\sigma^2)\): the density matrix is a Gaussian mixture of coherent states, hence mixed, not pure. Then average over \(I\) (Eq. 21).

Real-valued conventionThe paper takes real noise (one quadrature); \(\beta\) is real so \(\rho\) is a real symmetric matrix.
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Noise shifts the pulse a little at random, so the receiver no longer knows which state arrived.

Deterministic quadrature instead of Monte Carlo

The project quadrature oracle
\[ \rho_i\approx\sum_{a=1}^{n_I}\sum_{b=1}^{n_W}w_a\,v_b\;|\beta_{ab}^{(i)}\rangle\langle\beta_{ab}^{(i)}|,\qquad \beta_{ab}^{(i)}=\pm\sqrt{I_a\bar n}+\sigma x_b \]
  • \(w_a\)Gauss-Legendre in \(\log I\) times the GG density
  • \(v_b,x_b\)normalised Gauss-Hermite
  • \(n_I=48,n_W=41,d=16\)project grid and Fock cut-off

Then \(P_e=\tfrac12\bigl(1-\sum_k|\lambda_k(\tfrac12\rho_0-\tfrac12\rho_1)|\bigr)\) via eigvalsh, no SDP needed. The matrix truncated at \(d\) is renormalised to unit trace.

Checked propertyThe parity relation \(\rho_1=\Pi\rho_0\Pi\) with \(\Pi=\mathrm{diag}((-1)^n)\) holds to 1.1×10−16.
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Instead of drawing millions of samples we pick a few dozen well-chosen points and add them with weights.

Error probability conditioned on irradiance

From Eq. (36) to Eq. (38)
\[ P_{e,1}(\sqrt I\gamma+\epsilon)\overset{(37)}{\approx}\tfrac14e^{-4|\sqrt I\gamma+\epsilon|^2},\qquad P_{e,1}(I)=\mathbb E_\epsilon[\cdot]=\frac{1}{4\sqrt{1+8\sigma^2}}\;e^{-\frac{4I\gamma^2}{1+8\sigma^2}} \]
  • \(\gamma\)field amplitude, \(\bar n=\gamma^2\)
  • \(\sigma\)noise standard deviation
  • \(I\)fixed irradiance

Numerical check: \(I=1.3,\ \gamma=0.9,\ \sigma=0.7\) gives 0.04788053678 (formula) and 0.04788053678 (80-node Hermite integral).

Key stepThe Gaussian expectation of \(e^{-4(\sqrt I\gamma+\epsilon)^2}\) is a completing-the-square integral (App. C), which gives the compact exponential.
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
For fixed brightness the Gaussian noise is averaged out by one exponential formula.

The closed form Eq. (39)

Compact form with \(B=\tfrac{4\gamma^2}{1+8\sigma^2}\)
\[ P_{e,1}=\mathbb E_I\!\Bigl[\tfrac{e^{-BI}}{4\sqrt{1+8\sigma^2}}\Bigr]=\tfrac{1}{4\sqrt{1+8\sigma^2}}\!\int_0^\infty\! e^{-Bi}f_I(i)\,di \]
  • \(_1F_1\)confluent hypergeometric function
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Averaging \(e^{-BI}\) over the Gamma-Gamma law is its Laplace transform; the result is two \(_1F_1\) functions.

End-to-end error of the two-hop relay

Decode-and-forward relay (Eq. 12)
\[ P_e\approx P_{e,1}(1-P_{e,2})+(1-P_{e,1})P_{e,2}=P_{e,1}+P_{e,2}-2P_{e,1}P_{e,2} \]
  • \(P_{e,1},P_{e,2}\)errors of hop 1 and 2 (Eq. 39, 40)

Example: \(P_{e,1}=0.02,\ P_{e,2}=0.03\) give \(P_e=0.02\cdot0.97+0.98\cdot0.03=0.0488\).

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
The bit is wrong if exactly one hop is wrong; two wrong hops flip it twice and it is right again.

The mixed-state bound exceeds Eq. (39)

The gap between the three computations
\[ P_e^{(39)}\;\le\;\mathbb E\bigl[P_e^{\text{pure}}(I,\epsilon)\bigr]\;\le\;P_e^{\text{Helstrom}}(\bar\rho_0,\bar\rho_1) \]
  • \(\mathbb E[P_e^{\text{pure}}]\)Eq. (36): a receiver that knows \(I\) and \(\epsilon\)
  • \(\bar\rho_i\)averaged mixed states

A real receiver does not know \(I\) and \(\epsilon\), so it must discriminate the averaged states \(\bar\rho_0,\bar\rho_1\). At \(\sigma=1,\gamma=2\): 0.02741 < 0.03863 < 0.08621.

Consequence for Fig. 7The Monte Carlo of the paper samples formula (37) itself, so agreeing with Eq. (39) is natural. It tests the integral, not the mixed-state Helstrom bound.
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Extra side information lowers error; a real receiver lacks it, so its true error is larger.

The finite matrix holds only 16 photon levels; large noise pushes probability beyond them.

Parity and the pure limit

The parity relation of BPSK
\[ \rho_1=\Pi\,\rho_0\,\Pi,\qquad \Pi=\mathrm{diag}\bigl((-1)^n\bigr),\qquad [\rho_1]_{mn}=(-1)^{m+n}[\rho_0]_{mn} \]
  • \(\Pi\)photon-number parity operator

Because \(\langle n|{-}\beta\rangle=(-1)^n\langle n|\beta\rangle\). Consequence: a network need only learn \(\rho_0\); \(\rho_1\) follows. Numerical check: max difference 1.1×10−16, trace 1, smallest eigenvalue 3.36×10−12 (\(\ge0\): valid).

No-turbulence, no-noise limit (\(\alpha_{GG}=\beta_{GG}=4\cdot10^4,\sigma=10^{-4}\)): oracle 0.0350674 versus Eq. (5) 0.0350633.

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
The two states mirror each other through a sign flip of odd levels; know one and you know both.

Detailed notes

Theory and historical background

Two-state discrimination has a closed solution from Helstrom (1969, 1976): the minimum error is \(\tfrac12(1-\|p_1\rho_1-p_0\rho_0\|_1)\). For coherent states Dolinar (1973) proposed a feedback receiver reaching it, while Holevo (1982) and Wilde (2017) extend to mixed states. On the channel side, Andrews and Phillips (2005) and Al-Habash (2001) supply the Gamma-Gamma irradiance model. Bhatnagar, Arti and Bhatnagar (2025) join the two branches and add a decode-and-forward relay.

Plain words, technique, table, calculation

Plain words: two weak opposite-phase laser pulses look alike; the atmosphere makes them flicker and noise blurs them. Technique: the received state is the mixture \(\bar\rho_i=\mathbb E_{I,\epsilon}|\beta\rangle\langle\beta|\); the Helstrom bound is \(\tfrac12(1-\sum|\lambda_k|)\) with \(\lambda_k\) of \(\tfrac12(\bar\rho_0-\bar\rho_1)\). Table: the three computations in the slide "Eq. (39), the Eq. (36) average and mixed Helstrom". Calculation: see the examples below.

Example · Pure BPSK (quantum optics)

\(\bar n=0.5\): \(e^{-4\bar n}=e^{-2}=0.1353\); \(\sqrt{1-0.1353}=0.9299\); \(P_e=\tfrac12(1-0.9299)=0.03506\). The approximation \(\tfrac14e^{-2}=0.03383\) is off by -3.51%.

Example · Minimum power for \(P_e=10^{-3}\) (optical communications)

Solving \(\tfrac14e^{-4\bar n}=10^{-3}\): \(\bar n=\tfrac14\ln250=1.38\) photons per pulse. At 1.55 \(\mu\)m one photon carries \(1.28\cdot10^{-19}\) J, so the pulse needs about \(1.77\cdot10^{-19}\) J (illustration, losses ignored).

Example · Rytov and GG parameters (meteorology, illustration)

\(C_n^2=5\cdot10^{-14}\), \(\lambda=1.55\,\mu\)m, \(L=1200\) m: \(k=4.054\cdot10^6\) m\(^{-1}\), \(\sigma_R^2=1.23\,C_n^2k^{7/6}L^{11/6}=1.391\). Standard form: \(\alpha=4.091\), \(\beta=2.071\); scintillation index \(\sigma_I^2=0.8453\). A clear night (\(C_n^2\sim10^{-15}\)) scintillates far less.

Example · Two-hop relay (wireless network)

2400 m link, \(\gamma=1\): direct \(P_e=0.04766\), two 1200 m hops give \(P_{e,1}=P_{e,2}=p\) and \(2p(1-p)=0.0774\): worse. At \(\gamma=4\): 0.001623 versus 0.001386: the relay is better (Eq. 39, 40, 12; \(\sigma=0.1\)).

Example · Mixed bound versus Eq. (39) (receiver design)

\(\sigma=1,\gamma=2,\alpha=4,\beta=1.9\): Eq. (39) 0.02741, Eq. (36) average 0.03863, mixed Helstrom 0.08621. Designing from Eq. (39) the true error is 3.15 times the prediction (assumption: the receiver does not know \(I,\epsilon\)).

Example · Fock cut-off for the surrogate project

\(d=16\), \(\bar n=5,\sigma=1\): lost trace 0.0943, \(P_e=0.07757\) versus \(d=48\): 0.070223. The project accepts it since large \(\bar n\) is rare.

Pitfalls in exercises

  • Mixing \(|\alpha|\) and \(|\alpha|^2\): the squared overlap is \(e^{-4|\alpha|^2}\), not \(e^{-4|\alpha|}\).
  • Forgetting the \(\tfrac12\) or \(\tfrac14\) factor when using the approximation \(\tfrac14e^{-4\bar n}\).
  • Mixing \(\gamma\) and \(\bar n\): \(\bar n=\gamma^2\).
  • Evaluating Eq. (39) in float64: mpmath is needed for small \(\gamma\).
  • Using \(\sum\lambda_k\) instead of \(\sum|\lambda_k|\) in Eq. (26).
