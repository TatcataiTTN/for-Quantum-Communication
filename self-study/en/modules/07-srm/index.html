Foundations

The problem of discriminating non-orthogonal states

Mean error probability of a measurement \(\{\Pi_i\}\)
\[ P_e = 1-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\eta_i\,\langle\phi_i|\Pi_i|\phi_i\rangle \]
  • \(\eta_i\)prior probability of state \(i\)
  • \(\Pi_i\)measurement operator for outcome \(i\)

If the \(|\phi_i\rangle\) are orthogonal, \(\Pi_i=|\phi_i\rangle\langle\phi_i|\) gives \(P_e=0\). When they are not, no measurement separates them perfectly, so the task is measurement design (Eldar and Forney 2001, Sec. 1).

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Alice sends one of a few states and Bob must guess. The more alike they are, the easier to guess wrong; the measurement is how Bob looks at the channel.

Rank-one POVMs and the resolution of the identity

Resolution of the identity on the span of the states
\[ \sum_{i=1}^{m}\Pi_i=\sum_{i=1}^{m}|\mu_i\rangle\langle\mu_i|=P_{\mathcal U}\qquad\text{(Eldar–Forney, Eq. 1)} \]
  • \(|\mu_i\rangle\)measurement vector (need not be normalised or orthogonal)
  • \(P_{\mathcal U}\)projector onto \(\mathcal U=\mathrm{span}\{|\phi_i\rangle\}\), \(\dim\mathcal U=r\)

The paper considers rank-one POVMs for \(m\le n\) normalised vectors in an \(n\)-dimensional space (Sec. 2.1). The extra operator \(\Pi_0=I-P_{\mathcal U}\) has probability 0 and is dropped.

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
The outcomes of the measurement must add up to the whole space, otherwise their probabilities do not sum to 1.

The Gram matrix and linear independence

The Gram matrix encodes the whole geometry
\[ S=\Phi^{*}\Phi,\qquad S_{ij}=\langle\phi_i|\phi_j\rangle,\qquad S_{ii}=1 \]
  • \(\Phi\)the \(n\times m\) matrix whose columns are \(|\phi_i\rangle\)
  • \(r=\operatorname{rank}S\)dimension of \(\mathcal U\); linearly independent when \(r=m\)

For coherent states \(|\alpha_k\rangle\): \(S_{jk}=\exp\!\big(-|\alpha_j|^2/2-|\alpha_k|^2/2+\alpha_j^{*}\alpha_k\big)\). For \(m\) PSK states of amplitude \(\sqrt{\bar n}\), \(S_{jk}=\exp\!\big(-\bar n+\bar n\,e^{2\pi i(k-j)/m}\big)\), always invertible so \(r=m\).

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
The Gram matrix is a table of pairwise similarity. Everything about the SRM depends only on this table.

The SVD of the state matrix

Singular value decomposition (Thm 1)
\[ \Phi=U\Sigma V^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sigma_i\,|u_i\rangle\langle v_i|,\qquad \Phi^{*}\Phi=V\Sigma^{*}\Sigma V^{*} \]
  • \(\sigma_i^2\)non-zero eigenvalues of \(S=\Phi^*\Phi\)
  • \(|u_i\rangle,|v_i\rangle\)the \(U\)-basis (in \(\mathcal H\)) and \(V\)-basis (in \(\mathbb C^m\))

It is a "square root" of an eigendecomposition: \(|u_i\rangle\langle v_i|\) is a transjector mapping an eigenspace of \(S\) onto one of \(T=\Phi\Phi^*\) (Sec. 3.1).

QuantitySizeRole
\(S=\Phi^*\Phi\)\(m\times m\)Gram
\(T=\Phi\Phi^*\)\(n\times n\)same non-zero eigenvalues
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
The SVD splits a map into rotate, stretch, rotate. The SRM will keep the rotations and drop the stretch.

Optimality conditions of the minimum-error measurement

Holevo and Yuen optimality conditions (Eq. 78, 79)
\[ \Pi_i\,(W_j-W_i)\,\Pi_j=0\ \ \forall i,j,\qquad \Gamma-W_i\succeq 0\ \ \forall i,\qquad \Gamma=\sum_j\Pi_jW_j \]
  • \(W_i=\eta_i\rho_i\)weighted density operator
  • \(\Gamma\)Hermitian operator; the second condition is a matrix inequality

These conditions are necessary and sufficient for the minimum-probability-of-error measurement (MPEM) but rarely solvable in closed form. Typical use: test a candidate such as the SRM against them (Sec. 8.3).

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Like checking a solution by substituting it back: we need not find the answer, only decide if a proposed one is right.

The Helstrom reference for two states

Helstrom bound for two pure states
\[ P_e^{\mathrm{opt}}=\tfrac12\Big(1-\sqrt{1-4\eta_0\eta_1\,|\langle\phi_0|\phi_1\rangle|^{2}}\Big),\qquad \eta_0=\eta_1=\tfrac12\Rightarrow \tfrac12\big(1-\sqrt{1-|\langle\phi_0|\phi_1\rangle|^2}\big) \]

Coherent BPSK: \(\langle\alpha|{-}\alpha\rangle=e^{-2\bar n}\), so \(P_e^{\mathrm{opt}}=\tfrac12(1-\sqrt{1-e^{-4\bar n}})\). This is the reference for the SRM at \(m=2\); later slides show the SRM matching it exactly when \(\eta_0=\tfrac12\).

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
For two states the optimal answer is known. For many states it is not, so we need the SRM as an approximate solution with a formula.

Geometry of the least-squares measurement

The figure shows the idea: find measurement vectors \(|\hat\mu_i\rangle\) closest to the states \(|\phi_i\rangle\) while being orthonormal; \(|e_i\rangle=|\phi_i\rangle-|\mu_i\rangle\) is the error vector.

Squared error
\[ E=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\langle e_i|e_i\rangle \]
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Picture two perpendicular arrows placed as close as possible to the two state arrows.

The least-squares problem for measurement vectors

The least-squares problem (Eq. 4, 5)
\[ \min_{M}\ E=\operatorname{Tr}\big((\Phi-M)^{*}(\Phi-M)\big)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad MM^{*}=P_{\mathcal U} \]
  • \(M\)the \(n\times m\) matrix of measurement vectors \(|\mu_i\rangle\)

Expanding by the SVD, \(E=\sum_i\langle d_i|d_i\rangle\) with \(|d_i\rangle=\sigma_i|v_i\rangle-|a_i\rangle\) and \(\{|a_i\rangle\}\) orthonormal (Eq. 13). Since \(\{|v_i\rangle\}\) are orthonormal, the minimum is at \(|a_i\rangle=|v_i\rangle\) (Eq. 14).

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
We do not optimise the error probability (hard) but geometric closeness (easy), then prove the two agree when there is symmetry.

The LSM solution: keep the rotations, drop the stretch

LSM solution
\[ \hat M=\sum_{i=1}^{r}|u_i\rangle\langle v_i|=\Phi\big((\Phi^{*}\Phi)^{1/2}\big)^{\dagger} \]
Residual error (Eq. 18, 19)
\[ E_{\min}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}(1-\sigma_i)^2=r+m-2\sum_{i}\sigma_i \]
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Replace every singular value \(\sigma_i\) by 1 and you get the orthonormal measurement closest to \(\Phi\).

Definition of the square-root measurement

SRM (Hausladen et al.), explicit (Eq. 53) and implicit (Eq. 54) definitions
\[ M=\big((\Phi\Phi^{*})^{1/2}\big)^{\dagger}\Phi,\qquad\langle\mu_j|\phi_k\rangle=\big(S^{1/2}\big)_{jk} \]
For linearly independent states
\[ M=\Phi(\Phi^{*}\Phi)^{-1/2}\ \Rightarrow\ |\mu_i\rangle=\sum_j\big(S^{-1/2}\big)_{ji}|\phi_j\rangle,\qquad M^{*}M=I_m \]
  • \(S^{-1/2}\)inverse square root of the Gram matrix

So the SRM coincides with the LSM (Sec. 7). The measurement vectors are orthonormal, so it is an orthogonal measurement, not a general POVM.

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
The SRM needs a single computation on the Gram matrix: take the inverse square root, then mix the states.

Theorem 3: uniqueness of the SRM

  • \(r=m\): \(M=\Phi(\Phi^{*}\Phi)^{-1/2}\) is unique, \(M^*M=I_m\).
  • \(r\lt m\): the SRM is not unique; \(M_{\mathcal U}=P_{\mathcal U}M\) is.
Implicit condition
\[ \langle\mu_j|\phi_k\rangle=(S^{1/2})_{jk} \]
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
For independent states there is exactly one SRM. For dependent ones there are several but the part inside \(\mathcal U\) is identical.

Weighted SRM and weighted least squares

Priors as weights (Sec. 5, 7)
\[ |\phi_i^{w}\rangle=\sqrt{p_i}\,|\phi_i\rangle,\qquad \hat M_w=\Phi W(W^{*}\Phi^{*}\Phi W)^{-1/2} \]
Residual (Eq. 43)
\[ E^{w}_{\min}=2\sum_i\big(w_i-\sigma_i^{w}\big),\quad w_i=\sqrt{p_i} \]
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
If a state occurs more often, give it a louder voice by scaling its amplitude with the square root of its prior.

Linearly dependent states and Neumark’s theorem

Orthogonal measurement in a larger space (Eq. 27, 29, 33)
\[ \tilde M=\sum_{i=1}^{m}|u_i\rangle\langle v_i|=UZ_mV^{*},\qquad \hat\Pi_i=P_{\mathcal U}\tilde\Pi_iP_{\mathcal U},\qquad \tilde E_{\min}=E_{\min}+m-r \]
  • \(Z_r\)the \(n\times m\) matrix with identity block \(I_r\) top-left

When \(r\lt m\) there are at most \(r\) orthonormal vectors in \(\mathcal U\). The orthogonality constraint pushes the measurement vectors out of \(\mathcal U\); projecting them back gives the optimal POVM, in the spirit of Neumark’s theorem (Sec. 4.1).

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
With more outcomes than dimensions, borrow extra dimensions to place orthogonal measurements, then flatten back.

Peres–Wootters as a special case of the SRM

Peres and Wootters: three two-qubit states (Eq. 55, 56)
\[ |\phi_1\rangle=|aa\rangle,\ |\phi_2\rangle=|bb\rangle,\ |\phi_3\rangle=|cc\rangle,\qquad \hat M=\Phi(\Phi^{*}\Phi)^{-1/2} \]

The polarisations \(a,b,c\) are \(60^\circ\) apart (0°, 60°, 120°) with equal priors. The Peres-Wootters measurement is the SRM and is least-squares optimal (Sec. 7); the set is cyclic, so it also minimises the error probability (Sec. 8.5C).

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
A measurement once built for one example turns out to be a general formula.

Two-vector example in 2D: building the Gram matrix

Data of the two-state example (Eq. 46, 47)
\[ |\phi_1\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix},\quad |\phi_2\rangle=\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}-1\\\sqrt3\end{pmatrix},\quad S=\begin{pmatrix}1&-\tfrac12\\-\tfrac12&1\end{pmatrix} \]
  1. Eigenvalues of \(S\): \(\sigma_1^2=1.500,\ \sigma_2^2=0.500\) (that is \(\tfrac32\) and \(\tfrac12\)).
  2. \(S^{-1/2}=\begin{pmatrix}1.12&0.30\\0.30&1.12\end{pmatrix}\) (Eq. 51).
  3. \(\hat M=\Phi S^{-1/2}=\begin{pmatrix}0.97&-0.26\\0.26&0.97\end{pmatrix}\) (Eq. 49), columns \(|\hat\mu_1\rangle,|\hat\mu_2\rangle\).
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
The two arrows are 120 degrees apart so their inner product is \(-1/2\). Everything else follows from that number.

2D example: measurement vectors, residual and error probability

Orthonormal measurement vectors and error
\[ |\hat\mu_1\rangle=1.12|\phi_1\rangle+0.30|\phi_2\rangle,\quad |\hat\mu_2\rangle=0.30|\phi_1\rangle+1.12|\phi_2\rangle,\quad \langle\hat\mu_1|\hat\mu_2\rangle=0 \]

Residual \(E_{\min}=\sum_i(1-\sigma_i)^2=(1-1.2247)^2+(1-0.7071)^2=0.1363\). The SRM error with equal priors is \(P_e=0.0670\), equal to the Helstrom value \(\tfrac12(1-\sqrt{1-1/4})=0.0670\) because \(m=2\).

A trap when reading the paperSec. 6 says the LSM residual is "approximately 0.095". That matches the peak of Fig. 2 (\(E^w_{\min}(p{=}\tfrac12)=0.0964\), weights \(w_i=1/\sqrt2\)), while \(E_{\min}\) of Eq. (18) gives 0.136. Always state which measure is meant.
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
The two measurement vectors are perpendicular and each is tilted about 15 degrees from its state.

Every two-state set is geometrically uniform

The reflection generating the group (Eq. 102, 105)
\[ R=I-2\,\frac{|w\rangle\langle w|}{\langle w|w\rangle},\quad |w\rangle=|\phi_2\rangle-|\phi_1\rangle \]
Two-state Gram values (Eq. 104)
\[ \sigma^2(0)=1+a,\quad \sigma^2(1)=1-a,\quad a=\langle\phi_1|\phi_2\rangle \]
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Two arrows are always mirror images across their bisector, so the optimal measurement must be symmetric too.

The four-state geometrically uniform example

The group \(\mathbb Z_2\times\mathbb Z_2\) (Eq. 94)
\[ U_2=\mathrm{diag}(-1,1,-1,-1) \]

With \(|\phi\rangle=\tfrac12(1,1,1,1)^{*}\) and \(|\phi_i\rangle=U_i|\phi\rangle\), the Gram entries are \(\tfrac12\{2,-1,0\}\) (Eq. 96). The four states sum to zero, so they are linearly dependent (\(r=3\lt m=4\)).

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Each measurement vector is a sign-flipped copy of the first, exactly as the states are generated.

Geometrically uniform sets: the SRM from the Fourier transform

Singular values via Fourier (Eq. 70)
\[ \sigma(h)=m^{1/4}\sqrt{\hat s(h)},\qquad V=\mathcal F \]
Measurement vectors (Eq. 75)
\[ |\mu(g)\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt m}\sum_{h}\langle g,h\rangle^{*}|u(h)\rangle \]
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
For a set generated by a group the Gram has circulant structure, so a Fourier transform diagonalises it at once.

Proof that the SRM is optimal for GU sets

The SRM is the MPEM for GU sets (Thm 4, Sec. 8.3)
\[ \Pi_i=|\mu_i\rangle\langle\mu_i|\ \Rightarrow\ \Pi_i(W_j-W_i)\Pi_j=0,\qquad \Gamma-W_i\succeq0 \]
The key step (Eq. 91, 92)
\[ \langle u|T|u\rangle=\langle F|\Sigma|F\rangle\langle u|\Sigma|u\rangle-|\langle u|\Sigma|F\rangle|^{2}\ \ge\ 0 \]

The last inequality is Cauchy-Schwarz. Consequence: for a geometrically uniform set the SRM is both least-squares optimal and minimum-error, generalising Ban et al. (1997).

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
We test the two optimality conditions on the SRM; the second reduces to a familiar inequality.

A sufficient condition beyond geometric uniformity

Sasaki’s sufficient condition for any linearly independent set
\[ \big(S_w^{1/2}\big)_{ii}=c\ \ \forall i\ \Rightarrow\ \text{SRM}=\text{MPEM},\qquad \langle v^i|\Sigma|v^i\rangle=c \]

The condition concerns the diagonal of \(S^{1/2}\) (with \(\Phi_w=\Phi\,\mathrm{diag}(\sqrt{\eta_i})\)), hard to test directly. Via the SVD: if all entries of \(V\) have equal magnitude, \(\langle v^i|\Sigma|v^i\rangle\) is constant and the SRM is optimal. For GU sets \(V=\mathcal F\) has entries of equal magnitude (Sec. 8.3).

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
It is enough that the diagonal of \(S^{1/2}\) is constant; a symmetry group is not strictly required.

Three traps when computing the SRM

Trap 1: a matrix root is not an entrywise root\(S^{1/2}\) takes roots of the eigenvalues, not of the entries of \(S\); for complex \(S\) use \(V\sqrt{\Lambda}V^{*}\).
Trap 2: linearly dependent statesWhen \(r\lt m\) use the pseudo-inverse \(\dagger\); \(S^{-1/2}\) does not exist and the SRM is not unique (Thm 3).
Trap 3: mixing up success and error\(P_c=\sum_i\eta_i|(S_w^{1/2})_{ii}|^2\) and \(P_e=1-P_c\); forgetting the square or \(\eta_i\) gives wrong values.
The correct formula
\[ P_e^{\mathrm{SRM}}=1-\sum_{i}\big|(\tilde S^{1/2})_{ii}\big|^{2},\quad \tilde S=D\,S\,D,\ D=\mathrm{diag}(\sqrt{\eta_i}) \]
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Most mistakes in exercises come from these three places: wrong root, missing pseudo-inverse, or a forgotten square.

Unambiguous discrimination and the SDP of Eldar (2003)

The unambiguous framework (Eq. 2, 3, 5)
\[ \langle\phi_i|\Pi_k|\phi_i\rangle=p_i\delta_{ik},\quad \Pi_i=p_iQ_i,\quad Q_i=|\tilde\phi_i\rangle\langle\tilde\phi_i| \]
Reciprocal states
\[ \langle\tilde\phi_i|\phi_k\rangle=\delta_{ik},\quad \tilde\Phi=\Phi(\Phi^{*}\Phi)^{-1} \]
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
The measurement never guesses wrong: it either answers correctly or says "inconclusive". The price is the inconclusive probability.

Semidefinite programming and the dual conditions

Standard SDP and its dual (Eq. 20 to 30)
\[ \min_{p}\ -\sum_i\eta_ip_i\ \ \text{s.t.}\ \ \sum_ip_iQ_i\preceq I_r,\ p_i\ge0;\qquad \max_{X}\ -\operatorname{Tr}X\ \ \text{s.t.}\ \operatorname{Tr}(Q_iX)-z_i=\eta_i,\ X\succeq0,\ z_i\ge0 \]
Necessary and sufficient conditions (Eq. 29, 30)
\[ X\Big(I_r-\sum_ip_iQ_i\Big)=0,\qquad z_ip_i=0 \]

The problem is convex, so it converges to the global optimum in polynomial time; each iteration costs about \(O(m^4)\) with block-diagonal structure (Sec. 5).

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
An SDP is linear programming for matrices; the dual solution adds a certificate that the answer is optimal.

Three-vector example: the SDP drops one state

Three vectors, priors \(\tfrac13\) (Eq. 43)
\[ |\phi_1\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}\!\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{pmatrix},\ |\phi_2\rangle=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\!\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\0\end{pmatrix},\ |\phi_3\rangle=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\!\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\1\end{pmatrix} \]

Optimum (checked by SLSQP): \(p=(0,\tfrac16,\tfrac16)\), i.e. state 1 is dropped entirely; \(P_D=\tfrac19=0.111\). The paper lists \(0.17\) for the last two entries (Eq. 47).

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Sometimes the optimum prefers never answering for one state to raise the success rate on the others.

The EPM is optimal for the right priors

Equal-probability measurement (EPM) with \(p=\sigma_{\min}^2\) (Eq. 33)
\[ \Pi_i=\sigma_m^{2}Q_i,\qquad \text{(s=1)}\ \ \text{EPM optimal}\iff|v_i(m)|^2=\eta_i \]
  1. \(\sigma_i^2\) of \(\Phi\): 2.4315, 0.5000, 0.0685.
  2. The last row of \(V^{*}\) has squares 0.606, 0.197, 0.197, i.e. \(\approx(0.6,0.2,0.2)\).
  3. With \(\eta=(0.6,0.2,0.2)\) the EPM is optimal, \(p=\sigma_3^2=0.0685\) (paper: 0.07).
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
For any state set some priors make the EPM optimal; that is the surprising content of Theorem 2.

Geometrically uniform sets: the EPM is optimal for unambiguous discrimination

GU set: the EPM is optimal (Thm 4)
\[ \Pi_i=p\,U_i|\tilde\phi\rangle\langle\tilde\phi|U_i^{*},\qquad p=\lambda_{\min}(\Phi\Phi^{*})=\tfrac29 \]

\(\Phi\Phi^{*}=\tfrac29\,\mathrm{diag}(4,4,1,9)\), smallest eigenvalue \(\tfrac29\) (Eq. 64). Each state is identified correctly with probability \(\tfrac29\approx0.222\).

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Symmetry helps both problems: minimum-error detection (SRM) and unambiguous discrimination (EPM) both get closed answers.

Application direction for the turbulent FSO channel

SRM for mixed states (extension, not in the two papers)
\[ \rho=\sum_ip_i\rho_i,\qquad \Pi_i=\rho^{-1/2}\,p_i\rho_i\,\rho^{-1/2},\qquad P_c=\sum_ip_i\operatorname{Tr}(\Pi_i\rho_i) \]

In FSO communication each symbol \(i\) yields a mixed state \(\rho_i\) after averaging over Gamma-Gamma turbulence and receiver noise. The formula needs no optimisation, only matrix roots of size \(d\times d\) per sample. This is an assumed application direction that must be checked numerically before use.

Detailed notes

1. Plain words: what the SRM is

Picture each state as an arrow. To learn which arrow was sent we need a set of mutually perpendicular sights, one per answer. The SRM places the sights as close to their arrows as possible while staying perpendicular. Technically that is \(M=\Phi(\Phi^{*}\Phi)^{-1/2}\): take the Gram matrix, compute its inverse square root, then mix the states. With numbers: see the 2D example slide, where \(S^{-1/2}\) has entries 1.12 and 0.30.

2. Theoretical and historical background

Helstrom (1976), Holevo (1973) and Yuen, Kennedy, Lax (1975) gave optimality conditions for the minimum-error measurement, rarely solvable in closed form. Peres and Wootters (1991) hand-built a good measurement for three states; Hausladen and Wootters (1994) proposed the general "pretty good measurement"; Ban et al. (1997) proved it optimal for symmetric sets. Eldar and Forney (2001) gave a new characterisation (least squares) and proved optimality for every geometrically uniform set. Eldar (2003) turned to unambiguous discrimination and showed it is an SDP. All years come from the reference lists of the two papers.

3. Worked examples, step by step
Ví dụ · 1 · Optical communication: coherent BPSK (illustration)
  1. Take \(\bar n=0.5\).
  2. Gram: \(\langle\alpha|{-}\alpha\rangle=e^{-1}=0.3679\); eigenvalues \(1\pm0.3679\).
  3. \(P_c=\tfrac14(\sqrt{1.3679}+\sqrt{0.6321})^2=0.9649\).
  4. \(P_e=0.0351\), equal to Helstrom.
Ví dụ · 2 · Optical communication: coherent QPSK (illustration)
  1. \(M=4\), \(\bar n=0.5\); circulant \(4\times4\) Gram.
  2. Eigenvalues: 0.0505, 0.3033, 1.2137, 2.4324.
  3. \(P_e=1-\tfrac1{16}(\sum\sqrt{\lambda})^2=0.2617\).
  4. The set is cyclic so this is also the optimum (Thm 4).
Ví dụ · 3 · Quantum computing: reading a qutrit from three states (illustration)
  1. The three states of Eq. (43) in Eldar (2003) serve as three non-orthogonal labels with priors \((0.6,0.2,0.2)\).
  2. Weighted SRM: \(P_e=0.2746\).
  3. Numerical optimum: \(P_e=0.2440\).
  4. Gap 0.0306: with skewed priors the SRM is no longer optimal.
Ví dụ · 4 · Phase estimation: 8 discrete phases (illustration)
  1. The unknown phase is one of 8 equally spaced values (8-PSK), \(\bar n=2\).
  2. The set is cyclic so the SRM is optimal: \(P_e=0.2230\).
  3. Random guessing gives \(P_e=7/8=0.875\); the measurement cuts the error about 3.9-fold.
Ví dụ · 5 · Unambiguous discrimination of four GU states (numbers from the paper)
  1. \(\Phi\Phi^{*}=\tfrac29\mathrm{diag}(4,4,1,9)\) (Eq. 64).
  2. Smallest eigenvalue \(p=\tfrac29\).
  3. The inconclusive probability for each state is \(1-\tfrac29=\tfrac79\approx0.778\).
4. Case study and exam traps

Case: the three-vector example of Eldar (2003) shows the optimal SDP solution drops state 1 entirely (\(p_1=0\)); the EPM keeps all three but with much smaller probability (0.069 versus 0.167 per state).

  • Forgetting the pseudo-inverse for linearly dependent states.
  • Taking entrywise roots of the Gram instead of the matrix root.
  • Omitting \(\sqrt{\eta_i}\) in the weighted SRM.
  • Confusing \(|\alpha|\) and \(|\alpha|^2\) in \(\langle\alpha|\beta\rangle\).
