The M-ary Helstrom problem that needs a surrogate

Maximal success probability of an M-ary measurement
\[ P_{\text{succ}}=\max_{\{\Pi_i\}}\sum_{i=1}^{M}p_i\,\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_i\Pi_i),\qquad \Pi_i\succeq0,\ \ \sum_i\Pi_i=I,\qquad P_e=1-P_{\text{succ}} \]
  • \(\rho_i\)received state for symbol \(i\) (density matrix)
  • \(\Pi_i\)POVM
  • \(p_i\)prior probability

For \(M=2\) with equal priors the closed answer is \(P_e=\tfrac12\bigl(1-\tfrac12\|\rho_1-\rho_0\|_1\bigr)\). For \(M\gt2\) there is no general closed form, so one solves an SDP or uses an approximate measurement.

Question of the moduleWhich literature lets a fast evaluation layer replace the repeated SDP while staying close to the optimum?
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Telling several close states apart is like guessing a blurred card. The optimal guess can be found with an SDP, but each solve costs tens of milliseconds.

The pretty good measurement as a fast evaluation layer

Pretty good measurement (square-root measurement)
\[ \rho=\sum_i p_i\rho_i,\qquad \Pi_i^{\text{PGM}}=\rho^{-1/2}\,(p_i\rho_i)\,\rho^{-1/2} \]
  • \(\rho\)average state; inverse taken on the support
  • \(\Pi_i^{\text{PGM}}\)measurement computed directly from \(\rho_i,p_i\), no solver

PGM needs one eigendecomposition of \(\rho\) and a few matrix products, an \(O(d^3)\) cost per sample. Two groups of results in the literature tell when PGM is optimal and when it is not.

Zhou, Chessa, Chitambar, Leditzky, On the distinguishability of geometrically uniform quantum states, J. Phys. A 58, 415303 (2025), Eq. (3), p. 4.

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
PGM is a "square-root" measurement: no optimisation, just a matrix computation. Fast, but not always the best.

Geometrically uniform ensembles

Geometrically uniform (GU) ensemble and Proposition 8
\[ \rho_g=U_g\,\rho\,U_g^{\dagger}\ (g\in G,\ |G|=n),\qquad \sum_{g\in G}gXg^{\dagger}=\frac{n}{d}\,\mathrm{tr}(X)\,I,\qquad p^{*}_{\text{succ}}=\frac{d}{n}\,\lambda_{\max}(\rho) \]
  • \(G\)finite group acting irreducibly on a \(d\)-dimensional space
  • \(\lambda_{\max}(\rho)\)largest eigenvalue of the generator state \(\rho\)

A GU example: \(M\) coherent states \(|\alpha e^{2\pi ik/M}\rangle\) generated by the cyclic group \(\mathbb Z_M\). Holevo’s formula for \(p^*_{\text{succ}}\) needs only \(\lambda_{\max}\), not an SDP.

Zhou, Chessa, Chitambar, Leditzky, On the distinguishability of geometrically uniform quantum states, J. Phys. A 58, 415303 (2025), Eq. (24), (25), p. 8.

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Think of identical fan blades spaced evenly around an axis. That symmetry gives a compact answer.

PGM optimal for symmetric pure ensembles

Optimal success probability (Eq. 20)
\[ \begin{aligned}P_s^{\text{MPE}}&=\frac{1}{4N^2}\Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{4}c_k\sqrt{1+s_k}\Bigr)^2\\ c&=(1,1,N\!-\!1,N\!-\!1)\end{aligned} \]
  • \(s_k\)four linear combinations of \(e_1,e_2,e_3\) in Eq. (20)
  • \(e_1\)\(e^{-|\alpha|^2}\)

Every result of the paper uses pure states.

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
For symmetric pure states, the "square-root" measurement is the best one. The figure shows the gap between the optimal measurement and homodyne.

Necessary and sufficient conditions for SRM optimality

Double BPSK, \(p=q=1/4\)
\[ P_c=\tfrac1{16}\Bigl(\sum_{\pm,\pm}\sqrt{1\pm\eta\pm|\chi\pm\xi|}\Bigr)^2 \]
  • \(\eta\)\(e^{-2\alpha^2}\)
  • \(\chi,\xi\)\(e^{-\alpha^2(1-e^{i\delta})},\ e^{-\alpha^2(1+e^{i\delta})}\)

SRM is optimal if and only if \(G^{1/2}\) is block diagonal and each block has equal diagonal entries.

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
This paper gives a test: look at the Gram matrix to see at once whether the "square root" is optimal, without solving an SDP.

The family of \(\alpha\)-power-PGM measurements

\(\alpha\)-power-PGM
\[ M_i^{(\alpha)}=\tilde\rho^{-1/2}\,(p_i\rho_i)^{\alpha}\,\tilde\rho^{-1/2},\qquad \tilde\rho=\sum_i(p_i\rho_i)^{\alpha},\qquad \alpha\ge1 \]
  • \(\alpha=1\)PGM
  • \(\alpha=2\)quadratically weighted measurement
  • \(\alpha\to\infty\)approaches the optimal measurement for irreducible representations

The exponent \(\alpha\) is a continuous knob between the cheap PGM and the optimal measurement. The cost is still a matrix power and an inverse square root, with the same differentiable structure as PGM.

Zhou, Chessa, Chitambar, Leditzky, On the distinguishability of geometrically uniform quantum states, J. Phys. A 58, 415303 (2025), Eq. (4), p. 4.

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
The larger \(\alpha\), the more the measurement "concentrates" on the strongest direction of the generator state. \(\alpha=1\) is the ordinary PGM.

The closed form of success probability in alpha

Success probability of \(\alpha\)-PGM (Eq. 37)
\[ p_{\text{succ}}(\alpha)=\frac{d}{n}\,\frac{\mathrm{tr}[\rho^{\alpha+1}]}{\mathrm{tr}[\rho^{\alpha}]},\qquad \tilde\rho\ \propto\ I\ \ \text{(do Eq. 24)} \]
  • \(d,n\)space dimension, group order
  • \(\rho\)generator state, possibly mixed
  • Eq. (35): by irreducibility \(\tilde\rho\) is proportional to the identity, so \(\tilde\rho^{-1/2}\) is just a scalar.
  • Eq. (36): substitute and use Eq. (24) once more.
  • Eq. (37): what remains is a ratio of two traces, readable from the spectrum of \(\rho\).

Zhou, Chessa, Chitambar, Leditzky, On the distinguishability of geometrically uniform quantum states, J. Phys. A 58, 415303 (2025), Eq. (35) to (37), p. 9.

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
The spectrum of the generator state alone tells how well the \(\alpha\)-PGM does, with no measurement simulation.

PGM is suboptimal when the generator is mixed

Pure versus mixed
\[ \frac{\mathrm{tr}\rho^{\alpha+1}}{\mathrm{tr}\rho^{\alpha}}=\frac{\sum_k\lambda_k^{\alpha+1}}{\sum_k\lambda_k^{\alpha}},\qquad \lim_{\alpha\to\infty}p_{\text{succ}}(\alpha)=\frac{d}{n}\lambda_{\max}(\rho)=p^*_{\text{succ}} \]
  • \(\lambda_k\)eigenvalues of \(\rho\)
  • \(p_{\text{succ}}(\alpha)\lt p^*_{\text{succ}}\)for every finite \(\alpha\) when \(\rho\) is mixed
Generator\(\mathrm{tr}\rho^{\alpha+1}/\mathrm{tr}\rho^{\alpha}\)Conclusion
Pure (\(\rho^2=\rho\))\(1\)PGM (\(\alpha=1\)) is already optimal
Mixed\(\lt\lambda_{\max}\)PGM provably suboptimal
Trap: convergence only in the limitThe theorem says \(\alpha\to\infty\), not that every large \(\alpha\) is good. When \(\rho\) is nearly singular the inverse square root loses numerical stability (see the examples).
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Mixed means a "blurred" state. The blurrier the state, the farther the square-root measurement from the best one.

OptNet: a differentiable optimisation layer for QPs

A QP as a network layer
\[ z^\star=\arg\min_z\ \tfrac12z^{T}Qz+q^{T}z\ \ \text{s.t.}\ \ Az=b,\ Gz\le h \]
  • \(z^\star\)output of the layer
  • \((Q,q,A,b,G,h)\)learnable parameters

Backward goes through the KKT conditions by implicit differentiation, reusing the KKT factorisation from the forward pass. A batch of 128 QPs: 0.18 s versus 4.7 s for Gurobi (p. 6). Practical limit: below about 1000 dimensions (p. 5).

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
An optimisation layer is a "sub-network" that solves an optimisation problem and returns its answer. We can still take derivatives to train.

cvxpylayers: general convex layers, SDP included

Affine-solver-affine (ASA) form
\[ S(\theta)=R\bigl(\operatorname{solve}(C(\theta))\bigr),\qquad C,\ R\ \text{affine} \]
  • \(C\)maps parameters to cone-program data
  • \(R\)recovers the solution of the original problem

Every forward pass still calls a general conic solver. The library only adds derivatives and speeds up canonicalisation (pp. 2, 8).

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
cvxpylayers lets an SDP sit inside a network. It does not solve faster: it only knows the derivative.

Derivatives through the solution of an optimisation problem

Implicit differentiation through the optimality condition
\[ F(x^\star(\theta),\theta)=0\ \Rightarrow\ \frac{\partial x^\star}{\partial\theta}=-\Bigl(\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}\Bigr)^{-1}\frac{\partial F}{\partial\theta},\qquad \frac{d\mathcal L}{d\theta}=\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial x^\star}\,\frac{\partial x^\star}{\partial\theta} \]
  • \(F\)the KKT system or the cone-program optimality condition
  • \(\mathcal L\)network loss
  • Backward is a single linear system, much cheaper than forward (factorisation reused).
  • Unlike unrolling, no solver iterate is stored, so memory does not grow with iterations.
  • A regularity condition is required: a unique solution and an invertible \(\partial F/\partial x\).
Trap: differentiable does not mean fastThe forward pass of a differentiable SDP layer costs the same order as solving the SDP directly. Using it in the main training loop is as slow as calling the solver per sample.
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
How does the optimum move when a parameter is nudged? Implicit differentiation answers with one linear system.

Qubit example: optimum 0.4 versus PGM 0.34

A hand example: the Pauli group on one qubit
\[ G=\{I,X,Y,Z\}\ (n=4),\quad d=2,\quad \rho=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda,1-\lambda),\quad \rho_g=g\rho g^{\dagger},\quad p_i=\tfrac14 \]
  • \(\lambda=0.8\)mixed generator state
  • \(\lambda_{\max}\)0.8
  1. Optimum: \(p^*_{\text{succ}}=\tfrac dn\lambda_{\max}=\tfrac24\cdot0.8=0.4\).
  2. PGM (\(\alpha=1\)): \(\tfrac24\cdot\dfrac{0.8^2+0.2^2}{0.8+0.2}=\tfrac12\cdot0.68=0.34\).
  3. Gap: \(0.4-0.34=0.06\), so PGM loses \(15\%\) of the optimal success probability.

Computed by code: build the ensemble, compute the \(\alpha\)-PGM by matrix powers, matching the formula to 5.6e-17.

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
A case small enough to compute by hand. Four rotated states, and the optimal and PGM answers visibly differ.

Mixed states from phase randomisation

Four pure four-mode states (Eq. 34)
\[ \tilde P_{\text{corr}}^{\text{pure}}=\tfrac1{16}\Bigl(\sqrt{1+3e^{-4|\alpha|^2}}+3\sqrt{1-e^{-4|\alpha|^2}}\Bigr)^2 \]
  • \(|\alpha|=1\)0.9998
  • \(|\alpha|\to0\)\(1/4\)

Phase randomisation makes the states mixed and lowers \(P_{\text{corr}}\) at small \(|\alpha|\). The optimal way: count the total photon number, then apply PGM inside each block (p. 4).

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Randomising the phase makes states harder to tell apart at small amplitude. Because photon number is conserved, a clear optimal measurement still exists.

Cost of the differentiable SDP layer for M=4, d=16

Scale of the differentiable SDP layer
\[ \begin{aligned} m&=Md^2=4\cdot16^2=1,024,\quad \text{real}:\ M\tfrac{(2d)^2}{2}=2,048\\ t_{\text{batch}}&\approx B\,t_{\text{SDP}}=8\times65\ \text{ms}=520\ \text{ms}\end{aligned} \]
  • \(M,d\)symbols, truncated Fock dimension
  • \(B,t_{\text{SDP}}\)batch of 8, about 58 to 73 ms per sample (measured in the prototype)

For \(M=4,d=16\) the real variable count is about \(2048\), above the roughly \(1000\) dimensions OptNet itself calls practical for QPs (Amos and Kolter, p. 5). An SDP is dearer than a QP, so about \(0.5\) s per batch is a lower bound.

Label: estimate, not measuredThe 0.5 s per batch is the product \(8\times65\) ms from the archive notes. Time the real CvxpyLayer before relying on it.
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
A differentiable SDP layer is like hiring an expert to grade each paper: accurate, but slow for many papers. So use it only for the last fine-tuning stage.

Pitfalls and extensions

  • RL for measurements
  • State parametrisation and equivariance

The RL route that learns the measurement directly

The reinforcement-learning objective
\[ \max_{\pi}\ \mathbb E\Bigl[\sum_t\gamma^{t}r_t\Bigr],\quad r_T=P_{\text{succ}},\ \gamma=0.99 \]
  • \(\pi(a|s)\)policy choosing the next measurement
  • \(r_T\)final probability of a correct decision

RLNN learns the measurement strategy directly over several rounds, whereas the surrogate learns states and evaluates in one step.

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Instead of computing the best measurement by a formula, an agent tries many times and learns. The result matches the SDP, but it needs training.

The value and policy network architecture of RLNN

The two outputs of the network
\[ s\ \mapsto\ \bigl(V(s),\ \pi(a_1|s),\dots,\pi(a_{|A|}|s)\bigr),\qquad \sum_a\pi(a|s)=1 \]
  • \(|A|\)number of actions; binary measurements discretised to \(Q=20\) points on the Bloch sphere

Each branch has two hidden layers of 256 neurons with tanh activation (p. 5). The small deviation from the SDP comes from discretising the actions.

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
One branch estimates "how good is this situation", the other picks the action. Both share the input.

The scope limit of the RL route

Tensor-product states and local measurement constraint
\[ \rho_j=\bigotimes_{k=1}^{n}\rho_j^{(k)},\qquad P_{\text{succ}}^{\text{local}}\ \le\ P_{\text{succ}}^{\text{coll}}\ \text{(SDP)} \]
  • \(n\)number of qubit subsystems, up to 20
  • \(P^{\text{coll}}_{\text{succ}}\)collective measurement on the whole system

The multi-subsystem structure with one-subsystem-at-a-time measurement does not match the project’s single Fock system measured once.

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
RL here solves many qubits measured one by one. Our problem has one system measured once. The two problems differ in structure.

Parametrising density matrices with neural networks

Positive semi-definite parametrisation
\[ \hat\rho=AA^{\dagger},\quad A_{\sigma,a}=\langle\sigma,a|\psi\rangle,\quad \mathrm{rank}\,\hat\rho\le\dim\mathcal H_a \]
  • \(A\)raw Gram factor
  • \(\dim\mathcal H_a\)ancilla dimension, bounds the rank

The same spirit as the project’s Cholesky head: \(\hat\rho=LL^{\dagger}/\mathrm{Tr}(LL^{\dagger})\) gives a valid state by construction, without a penalty term.

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
To make a network always output a valid density matrix, let it output a matrix \(A\) and take \(AA^{\dagger}\). The result is always non-negative.

Equivariance for the cyclic rotation on the Fock space

Equivariance and twirling
\[ f(U_g\,x)=U_g\,f(x)\ \ \forall g\in G,\qquad \mathcal T(X)=\frac1{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}U_gXU_g^{\dagger},\qquad U_g=e^{2\pi i g\hat n/M} \]
  • \(\mathcal T\)twirling: projection onto operators commuting with the group
  • \(\hat n\)photon-number operator; \(\mathbb Z_M\) phase rotation on Fock

Nguyen et al. (PRX Quantum 2024) build a framework for equivariant quantum neural networks with an arbitrary symmetry group and discuss several ways to construct equivariant layers, such as nullspace, twirling and Choi operator.

Trap: quantum networks, not classical onesNguyen et al. treat equivariant quantum circuits. The project head is a classical network predicting density matrices, so the paper only supplies the concept.
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Equivariant means rotating the input rotates the output in the matching way. We build that into the network instead of making it learn it.

Designing the surrogate from alpha-PGM theory

Surrogate pipeline with an alpha-PGM evaluation layer
\[ x\xrightarrow{f_\theta}\hat\rho_i=\frac{L_iL_i^{\dagger}}{\mathrm{Tr}(L_iL_i^{\dagger})}\ \longrightarrow\ \tilde\rho=\sum_i(p_i\hat\rho_i)^{\alpha}\ \longrightarrow\ \hat P_e=1-\sum_ip_i\mathrm{Tr}\bigl(\hat\rho_iM_i^{(\alpha)}\bigr) \]
  • \(x\)channel parameters (amplitude, noise, \(C_n^2,L,\lambda\))
  • \(\alpha\)set to 3.5 by the sweep; could be learnable
  • Every step is a differentiable matrix operation (eigh, powers), so end-to-end training is possible.
  • No solver at inference: the cost stays \(O(d^3)\) per sample.
  • The residual between the \(\alpha\)-PGM and the SDP is the job of a correction head, about 11 times smaller than for plain PGM.

Design source: SYNTHESIS.md, Secs. 3 and 3.5. Speed measured in the prototype: about 0.06 ms per sample versus 58 to 73 ms for SDP, nearly 855 times.

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
The network predicts the states, then the "upgraded square root" formula derives the error probability. No SDP per sample any more.

Mixtures of coherent states over a variable-loss channel

The state averaged over a variable-loss channel
\[ \rho_x=\int_0^1 d\eta\,p(\eta)\,\bigl|\sqrt{\eta}\,\alpha_x\bigr\rangle\!\bigl\langle\sqrt{\eta}\,\alpha_x\bigr|,\qquad \alpha_x=(-1)^x a\in\mathbb R \]
  • \(\eta\)channel transmissivity, random
  • \(p(\eta)\)transmissivity distribution

Bilkis, Rosati and Calsamiglia (arXiv:2203.09807, 2022) optimise the error probability of Kennedy and Dolinar receivers averaged over the distribution of \(\eta\), and compute the Helstrom bound for mixtures of coherent states. Their abstract states this is a first, to the authors’ knowledge.

This is also the structure of the surrogate: mixedness comes from averaging a continuous random quantity, not from phase randomisation.

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
When the channel is sometimes strong and sometimes weak, the receiver sees a "mixture" of signals. That is the mixed state in the turbulence problem.

A real case: the PGM suboptimality theorem

Case thật · Zhou et al. (2025): provably suboptimal PGM

The paper moves from pure examples to mixed generators and general group representations. The main result for every finite \(\alpha\): the \(\alpha\)-PGM is strictly suboptimal for a mixed generator, and the success probability increases strictly with \(\alpha\).

\[ p_{\text{succ}}(\alpha)=\frac dn\frac{\mathrm{tr}[\rho^{\alpha+1}]}{\mathrm{tr}[\rho^{\alpha}]}\ \lt\ \frac dn\lambda_{\max}(\rho),\qquad \alpha\lt\infty \]
Zhou, Chessa, Chitambar, Leditzky, On the distinguishability of geometrically uniform quantum states, J. Phys. A 58, 415303 (2025), abstract, Eq. (37) p. 9, Sec. 4.2.
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
This is the mathematical evidence that the measured gap is not a modelling mistake.

Summary: four conclusions from the literature

Four conclusions from the literature
\[ \underbrace{P_e^{\text{PGM}}=P_e^{*}\ \text{(pure GU)}}_{\text{Krovi, Dalla Pozza}},\quad \underbrace{P_e^{\text{PGM}}\gt P_e^{*}\ \text{(mixed)}}_{\text{Zhou}},\quad \underbrace{P_e^{(\alpha)}\downarrow\ \text{in }\alpha}_{\text{Eq. (37)}},\quad \underbrace{\text{no free lunch}}_{\text{cvxpylayers}} \]
  • \(P_e^*\)optimal error (SDP)
  • The PGM-optimality theory covers pure states before the channel.
  • Averaging over turbulence yields mixed states, so the PGM gap is a mathematical consequence.
  • \(\alpha\)-PGM with \(\alpha=3.5\) closes about 11 times the gap, then turns because the inverse becomes unstable.
  • The differentiable SDP layer fits only the last fine-tuning step.
Still openThe \(\alpha\to\infty\) limit for the reducible representation (cyclic on truncated Fock) is absent from the papers read; Burenkov 2020 could not be read in full.
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
The literature gives us a reason to trust the gap, a cheap tool to narrow it, and a list of what nobody has done yet.

Detailed notes

In plain words

There are three ways to grade a hard multiple-choice test: hire an expert (SDP, exact but slow), use the quick "square-root" rule (PGM, instant but sometimes wrong), or train a student to guess (surrogate). The literature says when the quick rule suffices and how to improve it.

Theory and historical background

The square-root measurement (Hausladen and Wootters) is optimal for symmetric pure ensembles (Eldar and Forney; Krovi et al. 2015). Dalla Pozza and Pierobon (2015) give necessary and sufficient conditions. Zhou et al. (2025) prove that the \(\alpha\)-power-PGM converges to the optimum as \(\alpha\to\infty\) for irreducible representations. On the machine-learning side, OptNet (2017) and cvxpylayers (2019) place optimisation inside networks by implicit differentiation.

Worked examples

Example · Pauli qubit (quantum physics)
  1. \(\lambda=0.8\), \(d=2\), \(n=4\): optimum \(0.4\).
  2. PGM: \(0.5\cdot0.68=0.34\).
  3. \(\alpha=2\): \(\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{0.512+0.008}{0.64+0.04}=0.3824\).
  4. Gap left: \(0.0176\).
Example · Conditioning (numerical analysis)
  1. The spectrum of \(\tilde\rho\) scales as \((\lambda,1-\lambda)^{\alpha}\); condition number \(=(\lambda/(1-\lambda))^{\alpha}\) (toy-model assumption).
  2. \(\lambda=0.8\): \(\alpha=3.5\Rightarrow128\); \(\alpha=6\Rightarrow4096\); \(\alpha=16\Rightarrow4.3\times10^{9}\).
  3. With float32 (about 7 digits), \(\alpha=16\) is already meaningless.
Example · SDP variable count (optimisation)
  1. \(M=4,d=16\): \(m=4\cdot256=1024\).
  2. Real embedding: \(4\cdot32^2/2=2048\).
  3. Against the OptNet threshold of 1000: about twice as large.
Example · Break-even of the differentiable SDP layer (engineering economics, assumption)
  1. One epoch of 10 000 samples at \(65\) ms per sample: \(650\) s. (assumption)
  2. One epoch with \(\alpha\)-PGM at \(0.06\) ms: \(0.6\) s.
  3. Ratio \(\approx1083\); cvxpylayers is worth it only for a few final epochs.
Example · MIMO antenna design (illustration, same symmetry structure)
  1. An \(M=8\) PSK constellation under \(\mathbb Z_8\): \(n=8\). (illustration)
  2. With \(d=1\) per block, \(p^*=\tfrac dn\lambda_{\max}=\tfrac18\lambda_{\max}\).
  3. Checking the symmetry before running an SDP saves computation.

Case study

In the project SYNTHESIS.md, the \(\alpha\) sweep on mixed QPSK gives a gap of \(0.0711\) at \(\alpha=1\), \(0.0064\) at \(\alpha=3.5\), \(0.062\) at \(\alpha=6\). The old plan lacked this experiment; it was added after reading Zhou 2025.

Pitfalls in exercises

  • Forgetting the irreducibility condition when using Eq. (37).
  • Mixing up \(\mathrm{tr}\rho^{\alpha+1}/\mathrm{tr}\rho^{\alpha}\) with \(\mathrm{tr}\rho^{\alpha}\).
  • Treating a differentiable layer as a fast layer.
  • Using mis-named files (Brandsen, Koutny, DiAdamo) without checking content.
