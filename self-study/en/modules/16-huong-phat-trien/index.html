Why move to a time-dependent problem

Lindblad master equation for a lossy channel with thermal noise
\[ \frac{d\rho}{dt}=\kappa(\bar n_{th}+1)\Bigl(a\rho a^{\dagger}-\tfrac12\{a^{\dagger}a,\rho\}\Bigr)+\kappa\bar n_{th}\Bigl(a^{\dagger}\rho a-\tfrac12\{aa^{\dagger},\rho\}\Bigr) \]
  • \(\kappa\)loss rate
  • \(\bar n_{th}\)thermal photon number, mapped to the receiver noise \(\sigma^2\)
  • \(a\)photon annihilation operator

Paper 1 learns a static map from channel parameters to the final state, so all physics in between is squeezed into 36,000 SDP labels. The equation above describes that process exactly, so it can stand in for labels.

PositioningThe current network is not a PINN: its loss has only a Frobenius term on \(\rho\) and an MSE on \(P_e\), with no equation residual (direction-09 document).
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Instead of making the network memorise final answers, we tell it the rules (the equation) and let it work out the answers.

One trajectory covers the whole turbulence axis

\[ \eta=e^{-\kappa t},\qquad |\alpha\rangle\xrightarrow{t}\bigl|\alpha e^{-\kappa t/2}\bigr\rangle,\qquad t_I=-\frac{\ln(\eta_0 I)}{\kappa} \]
  • \(\eta_0\)initial transmissivity of the path
  • \(I\)one turbulence irradiance realisation
  • \(t_I\)effective time matching \(I\)

The Cholesky head along time

A valid state at every time
\[ \hat\rho(t)=\frac{L_\theta(t)L_\theta(t)^{\dagger}}{\mathrm{Tr}\bigl(L_\theta(t)L_\theta(t)^{\dagger}\bigr)},\qquad L_\theta(t)=\mathrm{TimeCholeskyNet}\bigl(t,|\alpha|,\kappa,\bar n_{th}\bigr) \]
  • \(L_\theta\)lower-triangular matrix, softplus on the diagonal
  • \(t\)time input added to the network
  • The Cholesky head is taken unchanged from paper 1, so \(\hat\rho(t)\) is valid even before the residual converges.
  • Existing PINN-Lindblad papers keep physics with penalty terms; here validity holds by construction along the whole trajectory.
  • The residual only multiplies \(16\times16\) matrices with the sparse \(a,a^{\dagger}\), is cheap, and autograd passes through it.
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
The network always returns a valid density matrix, because we take \(LL^{\dagger}\) and divide by the trace. The same trick was used in paper 1.

The cat qubit: encoding in a resonator

What a cat qubit is
\[ \begin{aligned}|0\rangle_\alpha&=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\bigl(|{+}\rangle_\alpha+|{-}\rangle_\alpha\bigr)\simeq|{+}\alpha\rangle\\ |1\rangle_\alpha&=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\bigl(|{+}\rangle_\alpha-|{-}\rangle_\alpha\bigr)\simeq|{-}\alpha\rangle\end{aligned} \]
  • \(|\pm\rangle_\alpha\)\(\mathcal N_\pm(|\alpha\rangle\pm|{-}\alpha\rangle)\)
  • \(\mathcal N_\pm\)\(1/\sqrt{2(1\pm e^{-2|\alpha|^2})}\)

A cat qubit is a qubit encoded in the coherent states \(|\pm\alpha\rangle\) of a resonator, named after Schrödinger’s cat. The mean photon number is about \(|\alpha|^2\).

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Picture a switch with two stable positions. The positions are two opposite coherent states. The further apart they are, the harder it is to flip the switch by accident.

The PINN-Lindblad loss

The three-term PINN-Lindblad loss
\[ \mathcal L=\underbrace{\|\hat\rho(0)-|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|\|_F^2}_{\text{IC}}+\lambda_{\text{PDE}}\,\frac1{N_c}\sum_j\Bigl\|\frac{d\hat\rho}{dt}\Big|_{t_j}-\mathcal L_{\text{Lind}}[\hat\rho(t_j)]\Bigr\|_F^2+\lambda_{\text{data}}\sum_{k}\|\hat\rho(t_k)-\rho_k^{\text{SDP}}\|_F^2 \]
  • ICinitial condition, known exactly, free
  • \(N_c\)number of collocation points, chosen freely, no labels
  • \(k\)a few anchor points with SDP labels
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
The initial condition and the equation supervise the network at every time without any labels. The costly labels only act as anchors.

The Lindblad residual and its verification

The time derivative in the residual
\[ \frac{d\hat\rho}{dt}\Big|_{t_j}\approx\frac{\hat\rho(t_j+h)-\hat\rho(t_j-h)}{2h},\qquad \mathcal L_{\text{Lind}}[\rho]=\kappa\Bigl(a\rho a^{\dagger}-\tfrac12\{a^{\dagger}a,\rho\}\Bigr)\ \ (\bar n_{th}=0) \]
  • \(h\)central-difference step, used to cross-check autograd
  • Autograd with respect to the input \(t\) (forward mode or differences) provides the derivative in the loss.
  • Ground truth: the analytic solution \(|\alpha e^{-\kappa t/2}\rangle\); thermal noise has a closed Gaussian solution.
  • Compare matrix element by element, no SDP needed.
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
The network derivative in time must match the right-hand side of the equation. The mismatch is the residual.

The phase-space variant

Wigner function and the Fokker-Planck equation
\[ \frac{\partial W}{\partial t}=\frac{\kappa}{2}\Bigl[\partial_x(xW)+\partial_p(pW)\Bigr]+\frac{\kappa(2\bar n_{th}+1)}{4}\bigl(\partial_x^2+\partial_p^2\bigr)W \]
  • \(W(x,p,t)\)Wigner function, a scalar field
  • \((x,p)\)continuous phase-space coordinates

In phase space the equation becomes the familiar diffusion problem of PINNs and needs no Fock truncation.

The nonlinear part is kept fixed and only the final linear part is learned. Learning the linear part means solving one linear system, which is very fast.

QRC: a quantum reservoir simulated classically
\[ \text{features}=\Bigl\{\langle\sigma_z^{i}\rangle,\ \langle\sigma_z^{i}\sigma_z^{j}\rangle\Bigr\},\qquad n+\binom n2\ \text{observables},\quad \dim\mathcal H=2^{n} \]
  • \(n\)qubits of the reservoir (6 to 10)
  • \(\sigma_z\)Pauli Z operator

The reservoir is a transverse-field Ising network or Kerr oscillators with fixed random parameters; the input is encoded in the local fields. The gate in document 07: a static QRC must beat NG-RC (\(7.4\times10^{-4}\)).

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
A small quantum system acts as a nonlinear mixer. We read off a few measurable quantities and then run a linear regression.

The two-photon dissipation mechanism

Two-photon dissipation
\[ \begin{aligned}L_2&=\sqrt{\kappa_2}\,(a^2-\alpha^2)\\ H_{\text{int}}/\hbar&=g_2\,a^{\dagger2}b+g_2^{*}a^{2}b^{\dagger}\\ \kappa_2&\approx4|g_2|^2/\kappa_b\end{aligned} \]
  • \(b,\kappa_b\)the buffer mode and its energy decay rate
  • \(\alpha^2=-\epsilon_d/g_2^{*}\)the buffer drive amplitude sets \(\alpha\)

A pair of resonator photons is exchanged for one buffer photon, so \(|0\rangle_\alpha,|1\rangle_\alpha\) and every superposition of them are steady states.

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
This mechanism acts like friction: any drift away from the two stable positions is pulled back, but the superposition between them is not destroyed.

The confinement condition of the cat qubit

Confinement condition
\[ \kappa_{\text{conf}}=2|\alpha|^2\kappa_2\ \gt\ \kappa_{\text{err}},\qquad |\alpha|^2\gt\frac{\kappa_{\text{err}}}{2\kappa_2} \]
  • \(\kappa_{\text{conf}}\)confining rate of the pseudo-potential
  • \(\kappa_{\text{err}}\)effective local error rate; in the experiment \(\kappa_{\text{err}}/2\pi=53\) kHz

With \(\kappa_2/2\pi=40\) kHz: \(|\alpha|^2\gt53/(2\cdot40)=0.66\), matching the "about 0.7" of the paper (p. 4).

Trap: errors must be local and weak enoughThe bit-flip only drops exponentially if \(\kappa_{\text{conf}}\gt\kappa_{\text{err}}\) for every dominant local error process; non-local errors still do damage.
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
The pull-back force must beat the noise. Then the state stays in the potential well, like a ball in a deep bowl.

Linear phase-flip rate
\[ \begin{aligned}\Gamma_{\text{pf}}&=\frac{2|\alpha|^2}{T_{1,\text{eff}}}\\ \frac{\Gamma}{2\pi}\Big|_{2.6}&=\frac{2\cdot2.6}{2\pi\cdot2.0\ \mu s}=0.41\ \text{MHz}\end{aligned} \]

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners

The risk of a parametric PINN

Balancing two loss terms
\[ \mathcal L=\mathcal L_{\text{IC}}+\lambda_{\text{PDE}}\mathcal L_{\text{PDE}}+\lambda_{\text{data}}\mathcal L_{\text{data}},\qquad \text{risk: }\ \lambda_{\text{PDE}}/\lambda_{\text{data}}\ \text{khó chỉnh} \]
  • \(\lambda\)weights; a wrong scale is the loss imbalance seen in paper 1
  • A parametric PINN (conditioned on many channel parameters) is harder to train than a single-system PINN; this is the main open risk.
  • Gate at prompt 3 of prototype_pinn/PROMPTS_12.md: extend from one trajectory to a network conditioned on \(\alpha\) and \(\kappa\).
  • The GradNorm experience of direction 00 can be reused.
Trap: a single trajectory does not prove the parametric PINNThe \(3.1\times10^{-3}\) error was measured on one fixed trajectory.
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Learning one path is easy. Learning a whole family of paths indexed by several knobs is far harder.

The weak point of the Wigner variant

Rebuilding \(\rho\) from the Wigner function
\[ \rho_{mn}=\pi\!\int\!W(x,p)\,\hat W_{mn}(x,p)\,dx\,dp,\qquad \Delta\rho\ \text{amplified in the tails of }W \]
  • \(\hat W_{mn}\)Wigner kernel of element \(mn\)
  • \(W\) may be negative so there is no "Cholesky for \(W\)"; the rebuilt \(\rho\) must be PSD.
  • Rebuilding \(\rho\) adds a layer of error before Helstrom; study the inverse map before promising results.
  • The decisive advantage: the output does not change for large \(d\), exactly the pain point of 8-PSK M-ary with \(d\ge48\).
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
The Wigner function avoids truncation, but in return we must convert back to a density matrix to compute Helstrom.

ESN gives a negative result on the static problem

Negative result of ESN on static input
\[ \text{MSE}_{\text{ESN}}(200)=6.49\times10^{-3}\ \gg\ \text{MSE}_{\text{NG-RC}}(200)=7.4\times10^{-4},\qquad \frac{6.49\times10^{-3}}{7.4\times10^{-4}}\approx8.8 \]
  • \(\text{ESN}\)300-node reservoir, input held constant for 50 steps
  • A random reservoir adds no information to a static problem, only high-dimensional noise.
  • The value of a reservoir lies in time-dependent problems, so the role of ESN/QRC moves to the stage combined with direction 08.
  • Every backbone comparison must have ridge-raw as a floor baseline.
Trap: boasting without a floor baselineLinear ridge on raw features already reaches \(1.1\times10^{-3}\) with only 30 samples.
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
A reservoir helps only when data has a time order. With a static input it just adds clutter.

GHDO: parametrising mixed states with deep networks

A mixed state with bounded rank
\[ \begin{aligned}\hat\rho&=AA^{\dagger},\quad \mathrm{rank}\,\hat\rho\le R^{K}\\ \langle\sigma|\hat\rho|\eta\rangle&=\prod_{h=1}^{K}\sum_{a=1}^../../../\psi^{(h)}_{\sigma,a}\bigl[\psi^{(h)}_{\eta,a}\bigr]^{*}\end{aligned} \]
  • \(R,K\)local rank and number of plaquettes

The GHDO is a deep network encoding positive semi-definite density operators of exponential rank with polynomial resources. The same spirit as the project Cholesky head.

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
For many spins the density matrix is too big to store. The GHDO builds it from small pieces yet keeps it always valid.

The dissipative Ising model as a benchmark for mixed states

The dissipative Ising model
\[ \begin{aligned}\hat H&=\tfrac V4\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\hat\sigma^z_i\hat\sigma^z_j+\tfrac g2\sum_i\hat\sigma^x_i\\ \hat L_i&=\sqrt\gamma\,\hat\sigma_i^{-},\quad S_2=-\log_2\mathrm{Tr}[\hat\rho^{\dagger}\hat\rho]\end{aligned} \]
  • \(V/\gamma=2\)paper parameters, a 16-site chain
  • \(S_2\)Rényi-2 entropy, measuring mixedness
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
This paper tests the idea on a dissipative model, much as we use Lindblad for the channel.

Bit-flip saturation and the experiment limits

When the bit-flip trade-off stops
\[ T_{\text{bit-flip}}^{\text{sat}}\approx1\ \text{ms}\ \text{for}\ |\alpha|^2\ge5,\qquad \kappa_{\text{conf}}\gt\kappa_{\text{err}}\ \text{fails}\Rightarrow\text{no exponential gain} \]
  • \(\chi_{qa}\)dispersive coupling of the cat qubit to the tomography transmon
  • The authors attribute the 1 ms saturation to the coupling to the tomography transmon (thermal occupation 1%, \(T_{1,q}=5\ \mu s\)).
  • In earlier experiments the bit-flip suppression was blocked because the circuit itself damaged the resonator (Methods).
  • Thermal photon in the buffer: \(\kappa_2\alpha^2\gg\chi_{ba}\) (condition 1) or \(\chi_{ba}/\kappa_b\ll\pi/2\) (condition 2); this experiment meets condition 2.
Trap: saturation is not a limit of the cat qubitThe saturation comes from the measuring device, not from the principle.
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
The exponential climb stops not because the idea fails but because the instrument used to look at the state disturbs it.

A real case: cat qubits suppress bit-flips exponentially

Case thật · Lescanne et al. (2020): hardware design for biased noise

The group places the cat qubit in a superconducting resonator and adds two-photon dissipation through a new nonlinear dipole (the ATS). Result: a bit-flip time of about 1 ms, 300 times the single-photon lifetime \(T_1=3.0\ \mu\)s, while the phase-flip only grows linearly.

\[ \underbrace{T_{\text{bit-flip}}\propto4.2^{|\alpha|^2}}_{\text{exponential}},\qquad \underbrace{\Gamma_{\text{phase-flip}}=2|\alpha|^2/T_{1,\text{eff}}}_{\text{linear}} \]
Lescanne et al., Exponential suppression of bit-flips in a qubit encoded in an oscillator, Nature Physics 16, 509 (2020), abstract; Figs. 3, 4 (pp. 3, 4); Methods.
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
The example shows that with the right design one error type can be almost fully suppressed. The price is that the other type grows slowly.

Detailed notes

In plain words

There are three ways to need fewer labels: tell the network the rules (PINN-Lindblad), use a simple model that needs few labels (reservoir), and pick the right points to ask (active learning). The cat qubit lives in another field, but it shows a similar idea: design things so that one error type vanishes by itself.

What a cat qubit is

A cat qubit encodes one qubit in two opposite coherent states \(|\alpha\rangle\) and \(|{-}\alpha\rangle\) of a resonator (a container for microwave light), named after Schrödinger’s cat because a superposition of the two states is a cat state. A two-photon dissipation pulls any drift back to these two states without destroying the superposition between them. The bit-flip error (a jump from \(|\alpha\rangle\) to \(|{-}\alpha\rangle\)) drops exponentially in \(|\alpha|^2\), while the phase-flip only grows linearly.

Theory and historical background

Lescanne et al. (2020) cite Shor (2001) for quantum error correction, Guillaud and Mirrahimi (2013) for repetition cat qubits, Leghtas et al. (2015) and Touzard et al. (2018) for confinement by two-photon dissipation. On the machine-learning branch, Vicentini et al. (2022) parametrise density operators with deep networks. Directions 07 and 08 of the project link these branches to the Helstrom problem.

Worked examples

Example · Semigroup (quantum physics)
  1. \(\alpha=1.5,\ \kappa=1,\ t=0.7\Rightarrow\eta=e^{-0.7}=0.4966\).
  2. \(\alpha(t)=1.5\sqrt\eta=1.0570\).
  3. Remaining photon number: \(|\alpha(t)|^2=1.1173\), equal to \(2.25\eta\).
Example · Turbulence coverage (communication engineering, assumption)
  1. \(\eta_0=0.25,\ \kappa T=2\). (assumption)
  2. \(I_{\min}=e^{-2}/0.25=0.541\), \(I_{\max}=4\).
  3. A realisation \(I=5\) lies outside the range.
Example · Confinement condition (device physics)
  1. \(\kappa_{\text{err}}/2\pi=53\) kHz, \(\kappa_2/2\pi=40\) kHz.
  2. \(|\alpha|^2\gt53/80=0.662\).
  3. The paper says "about 0.7", matching.
Example · Biased noise (coding theory)
  1. \(|\alpha|^2=5\): \(T_{\text{phase}}=1\ \text{ms}/10=100\ \mu\)s.
  2. Bias ratio \(=1\ \text{s}/100\ \mu\text{s}=10^4\) (projection).
  3. Only a one-dimensional repetition code is needed for phase-flips.
Example · Label budget (computational economics)
  1. \(36,000\times85.24\) ms \(=51.1\) minutes.
  2. 5% = 1800 labels, 2.6 minutes. (assumed target)
  3. At \(M=4,d=32\) each label is about 100 times dearer (document 05).
