Two families of automated geometry solving

Algebraic family: proof by polynomials
\[ g\in\sqrt{\langle h_1,\dots,h_k\rangle}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad h_1=\dots=h_k=0\ \Rightarrow\ g=0 \]
  • \(h_i\)hypotheses written as polynomials
  • \(g\)the conclusion to prove
  • \(\sqrt{I}\)radical of the ideal
Synthetic family: deduction closure
\[ F_{t+1}=F_t\cup\{\,r(F_t)\;:\;r\in\text{rules}\,\},\qquad \text{stop when } F_{t+1}=F_t \]
  • \(F_t\)facts derived by step t
FamilyExamplesTrait
AlgebraicWu, area method, Gröbnersure but hard to read
SyntheticDeduction database, full angleclose to human proofs
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
One family turns geometry into algebra and computes; the other deduces step by step like a student. AlphaGeometry picks the second family (AlphaGeometry2, Sec. 1).

Task taxonomy for geometry problem solving

Two common metrics
\[ \mathrm{Acc}=\tfrac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\mathbf 1[\hat y_i=y_i],\qquad \text{Top-}k\ \mathrm{Acc}=\tfrac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\mathbf 1[y_i\in\hat Y_i^{(k)}] \]
  • \(\hat Y^{(k)}_i\)the k top-ranked answers
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Geometry has three layers: understanding the figure, proving or computing, then general math reasoning. Each layer has its own datasets.
Proof: AAS for two triangles
\[ \angle BAC=\angle CAD,\ \ \angle B=\angle D,\ \ AC=AC\ \Rightarrow\ \triangle ABC\cong\triangle ADC\ \Rightarrow\ BC=CD \]

DDAR and the deduction closure

DDAR computes the deduction closure
\[ \mathrm{cl}(F_0)=\bigcup_{t\ge0}F_t,\qquad F_{t+1}=F_t\cup\mathrm{DD}(F_t)\cup\mathrm{AR}(F_t) \]
  • \(\mathrm{DD}\)geometric deduction rules (deductive database)
  • \(\mathrm{AR}\)linear arithmetic over angles and lengths (algebraic reasoning)

When \(\mathrm{cl}(F_0)\) lacks the conclusion, the language model proposes an auxiliary construction and DDAR runs again. AlphaGeometry2 stresses that DDAR drives both training-data generation and proof search.

RememberThe set \(F_t\) only grows and is finite, so the loop always stops.
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Like flood fill: from a few starting facts keep applying rules to add new facts until nothing more can be added.

The domain language and geometric predicates

The AlphaGeometry domain language
\[ \texttt{cong}:AB=CD,\quad \texttt{perp}:AB\perp CD,\quad \texttt{eqratio}:\tfrac{AB}{CD}=\tfrac{EF}{GH} \]
  • \(\texttt{cyclic}\)four points on one circle
  • \(\texttt{eqangle}\)two directed angles are equal
New linear predicates of AG2
\[ \texttt{distmeq}:\ \sum_{i=1}^n t_i\log(A_iB_i)+y=0,\qquad \texttt{distseq}:\ \sum_{i=1}^n t_iA_iB_i=0 \]

AG1 has 9 basic predicates covering 66% of IMO 2000 to 2024 problems. AG2 adds acompute, rcompute, distmeq, distseq, angeq and 11 locus types, raising coverage to 88%.

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
A machine understands a figure only when it is written as short sentences like "AB equals CD". AG2 teaches it new sentence types.

DDAR2 is over 300 times faster

Speed-up of DDAR2 over DDAR1
\[ S=\frac{t_{\mathrm{DDAR1}}}{t_{\mathrm{DDAR2}}}=\frac{1179{,}57\ \mathrm{s}}{3{,}44711\ \mathrm{s}}=342.2 \]
  • \(t\)mean time over 25 IMO problems DDAR cannot solve, 50 runs
Theoretical cost of similar-triangle search
\[ T_{\mathrm{AG1}}=O(N^8)\ \longrightarrow\ \text{AR queries}\ \le O(N^3)\ \text{(AG2)} \]
  • \(N\)number of points in the problem

Hashing triangle "shapes" and (A, B, angle AXB) for cyclic quadrilaterals replaces exhaustive search; Gaussian elimination is rewritten in C++ through pybind11.

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Instead of trying every combination of points, DDAR2 gives each shape a "fingerprint" and compares matching fingerprints only.

Synthetic data from random diagrams

Share of proofs with and without auxiliary points
\[ \text{AG1}:\ 9:91,\qquad \text{AG2}:\ 50:50,\qquad |\mathcal D_{\mathrm{AG2}}|\approx3\times10^{8}\ \text{theorem} \]
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
The system draws random figures, deduces every fact, then traces back to get problems with the shortest proofs. No human-written problems needed.

Minimising a problem with a greedy algorithm

Greedy point pruning
\[ \mathrm{pruned}\leftarrow\mathrm{pruned}\setminus\{p\}\ \text{ if }\ \mathrm{check\_provable}(\mathrm{pruned}\setminus\{p\})\ \text{ holds} \]
  • \(p\)taken in reverse topological order

Enumerating all subsets to find the minimal problem costs \(2^{|P|}\); the greedy algorithm needs only \(|P|\) checks if the check is monotone. The construction-dependency closure breaks monotonicity, hence the reverse topological order.

Try dropping each point; if the problem is still provable, drop it for good. This takes as many tries as there are points.

LeanGeo-Bench and the pass@k metric

Unbiased pass@k estimator
\[ \mathrm{pass@}k=1-\frac{\binom{n-c}{k}}{\binom{n}{k}},\qquad n=4,\ c=1:\ \ k=1\to0.25,\ k=2\to0.5,\ k=4\to1 \]
  • \(n\)samples generated per problem
  • \(c\)number of correct samples

Aristotle: Monte Carlo search over proof graphs

The PUCT rule (standard form)
\[ a^\star=\arg\max_a\Bigl[Q(s,a)+c\,P(a\mid s)\,\frac{\sqrt{N(s)}}{1+n(s,a)}\Bigr] \]
  • \(P\)prior from the generative policy
  • \(Q\)estimated value

Example \(c=1.5,N=100\): \((Q,P,n)=(0.5;0.6;10)\to1.318\); \((0.6;0.1;30)\to0.6484\); \((0;0.3;0)\to4.5\): the untried branch is preferred.

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Each proof step is a "move". PUCT balances moves that already look good against moves barely tried.

MagicGeo: from statement to coordinates to TikZ

Constraints into coordinates
\[ O=(0,0),\ B=(-1,0),\ |OC|=1,\ \angle BOC=66^\circ\ \Rightarrow\ C=(\cos246^\circ,\sin246^\circ)=(-0.407,-0.914) \]
  • \(C\)matches the coordinates (-0.41, -0.91) printed in the figure

The LLM only translates the statement into points and constraints; a numeric solver finds coordinates satisfying them; only then is the figure drawn. Hence no wrong measures.

Weighted segmentation loss
\[ L_{bs*}=\frac{-w_*}{M_{map}}\sum_{i=1}^{M_{map}}\bigl[y^*_i\log p^*_i+(1-y^*_i)\log(1-p^*_i)\bigr],\quad w_p=5,\ w_l=1,\ w_c=4 \]
  • \(*\)point, line or circle

SVAMP exposes shortcuts in older datasets

Drop when moving to the challenge set
\[ \Delta=\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{MAWPS}}-\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{SVAMP}}=88.7-43.8=44.9\ \text{points},\qquad \tfrac{43.8}{88.7}=0.494 \]
  • \(\mathrm{Graph2Tree\,(R)}\)best model, with RoBERTa
Trap: models learn shortcuts, not reasoningRemove the question entirely and models still score high; a bag-of-words model with no word order also scores high on MAWPS and ASDiv-A (Patel et al., 2021).
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
If a few changed words halve the score, the model did not understand the problem.

Language coverage versus solve rate

Confusing "unsolvable" with "language not extended"
\[ \underbrace{66\%\to88\%}_{\text{language coverage}}\ \ne\ \underbrace{54\%\to84\%}_{\text{solve rate}} \]
  • \(12\%\)remaining: 3D, inequalities, non-linear equations, arbitrary number of points

Of 50 problems, \(0.84\times50=42\) are solved; 6 cannot be formalised; 2 were attempted but unsolved (IMO 2018 P6 and 2023 P6 need inversion or projective geometry).

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Coverage is "the problem can be written in the machine language"; solve rate is "the machine produces a proof". They differ.

Benchmark size changes the conclusion

Trusting the wrong benchmark: AG-30 versus AG-231
\[ \Delta_{30}=83.3-60.0=23.3,\qquad \Delta_{231}=98.7-92.2=6.5\ \text{(percentage points)} \]
  • \(\Delta\)gain of the LLM over DDAR + heuristics
Trap: a small set inflates the gainNewclid recomputes: on 30 problems the LLM leads by about 23 points, on 231 only about 6.5; the 231-problem set also lacks a published collection protocol (Sicca et al., 2024).
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
With 30 problems each is worth 3.3%. The conclusion about LLMs depends strongly on the set used.

The strongest LLMs reach only about 27% pass@4 and solve no Olympiad-level problem in LeanGeo-Bench (Song et al., 2025, Table 3). Natural proofs skip diagram conditions ("the figure shows") that Lean demands explicitly.

Trap: the figure is hidden evidenceProving by eye on a figure can fail for another configuration (point order, coincident points). AG2 adds sameclock and noverlap predicates to check this.

Implicit diagrams and formal verification

Gap between natural and formal
\[ \Pr[\text{formal}\mid\text{informal}]\ \ll\ 1,\qquad \text{LeanGeo-Bench}:\ \tfrac{62}{122}=50.8\%\ \text{are Olympiad level} \]
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
A person sees it in the figure; a machine must be told every condition.

Extension: two-way neuro-symbolic coupling

Training objective after distilling from the engine
\[ \mathcal L(\theta)=-\sum_{(x,\pi)\in\mathcal D_{\mathrm{sym}}}\sum_t\log p_\theta(\pi_t\mid x,\pi_{\lt t}) \]

Detailed notes

Theory and historical background

Machine proving of geometry theorems has two lines: algebraic (Wu, area method, Gröbner) and synthetic (deduction database, full angle). AlphaGeometry (2024) pairs a language model with the DDAR engine; AlphaGeometry2 (2025) widens the language, speeds the engine by over 300 times and adds SKEST search. In parallel Lean 4 and mathlib allow formal verification, while surveys (Ma et al., Zhao et al., 2025) classify tasks, data and methods.

Plain words, technique, table, calculation

Plain words: the machine draws random figures, deduces every true fact, then learns which extra points to add. Technique: \(F_{t+1}=F_t\cup\mathrm{DD}(F_t)\cup\mathrm{AR}(F_t)\); the language model proposes an auxiliary construction when the closure lacks the conclusion. Table: Table 4 of AG2. Calculation: the examples below.

Example · Geometry: the midline (mathematics)

\(A=(0,0),B=(2a,0),C=(2b,2c)\), \(M,N\) midpoints of \(AB,AC\) with \(a=1.3,b=0.7,c=1.1\): the cross product \(\vec{MN}\times\vec{BC}=0\) so \(MN\parallel BC\), ratio \(|MN|/|BC|=0.5\).

Example · Search cost (computer science)

Beam \(128\), depth \(4\), \(32\) samples: \(128\cdot32=4096\) samples per level; \(4\) levels give at most \(4\cdot4096=16384\) model calls (rough count, assumption).

Example · pass@k (model evaluation)

One problem, \(n=10\) samples, \(c=3\) correct: pass@1 \(=0.3\), pass@5 \(=0.9167\). Using more samples raises the metric although the model is unchanged.

Example · Education: grading a geometry answer (illustration)

A class of 40 students submits proofs; a grader compares only the number for calculation problems (\(2827.43\)) but must check each step of a proof. Assume 6 steps and step accuracy \(0.95\): the whole proof is right with \(0.95^6=0.735\).

Example · Linguistics: sampling temperature (illustration)

Logits \((2,1,0.1)\): \(T=1\) gives top probability \(0.659\); \(T=0.5\) gives \(0.864\); \(T=2\) gives \(0.502\). A higher temperature flattens the distribution.

Example · Software performance (illustration)

If a run takes \(1179.57\) s and the new version \(3.44711\) s, 1000 runs save \(1000\cdot(1179.57-3.44711)/3600=326.7\) hours.

Pitfalls in exercises

  • Mixing language coverage (66% to 88%) with solve rate (54% to 84%).
  • Forgetting \(\binom{n-c}{k}/\binom{n}{k}\) and using \(c/n\) for pass@k.
  • Multiplying the mean times and taking it for a new measurement.
  • Reading 110 papers in 2024 from the text instead of 111 from the chart.
  • Assuming a high score on an older benchmark proves the model understands the problem.
