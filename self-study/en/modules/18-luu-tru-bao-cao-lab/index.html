Coherent states in the quantum-optics textbook

A coherent state is an eigenvector of the annihilation operator (Eq. 3.2)
\[ \hat a|\alpha\rangle=\alpha|\alpha\rangle,\qquad |\alpha\rangle=e^{-|\alpha|^2/2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha^n}{\sqrt{n!}}|n\rangle \]
  • \(\alpha\in\mathbb C\)complex amplitude, encodes amplitude and phase
  • \(\bar n=|\alpha|^2\)mean photon number
Photon-number law
\[ P(n)=e^{-\bar n}\frac{\bar n^{\,n}}{n!},\qquad P(0)\big|_{\bar n=0.5}=e^{-0.5}=0.6065 \]
  • \(P(n)\)Poisson, mean and variance both equal \(\bar n\)

The Gerry and Knight (2005) textbook in the archive has 333 pages; chapter 3 (pp. 43 to 73) was read for this module.

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
A weak laser pulse is a Poisson "bag of photons". A coherent state records exactly that.

Helstrom bound and the BPSK case (Steps 3 to 5)

  • \(|\psi_0\rangle=|{+}\gamma\rangle,\ |\psi_1\rangle=|{-}\gamma\rangle\)the two BPSK states
  • \(\gamma^2=\bar n\)mean photon number

The Gamma-Gamma channel in the lab M-ary slides

Rytov variance and Gamma-Gamma parameters (Steps 7 to 9)
\[ \sigma_R^2=1.23\,C_n^2k^{7/6}L^{11/6},\qquad \alpha=\Bigl[e^{\frac{0.49\sigma_R^2}{(1+1.11\sigma_R^{12/5})^{7/6}}}-1\Bigr]^{-1} \]
  • \(k=2\pi/\lambda\)wavenumber
  • \(C_n^2\)turbulence strength

Example \(C_n^2=5\cdot10^{-14}\), \(\lambda=1.55\,\mu\)m, \(L=1200\) m: \(\sigma_R^2=1.391\), the standard form gives \(\alpha=4.091\), \(\beta=2.071\).

RememberThe Rytov variance grows as \(L^{11/6}\): splitting a 4 km link into two 2 km hops cuts \(\sigma_R^2\) per hop by \(2^{11/6}=3.56\) (the deck p. 8 says "about 3.9 times").
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
The more turbulent the air, the more the received light flickers; alpha and beta describe that flicker.

The two-hop relay in the lab diagram

End-to-end error of the decode-and-forward relay
\[ P_e=P_{e,1}(1-P_{e,2})+(1-P_{e,1})P_{e,2},\qquad (0.02,0.03)\mapsto0.0488\ \ (\approx P_{e,1}+P_{e,2}=0.05) \]
  • \(P_{e,1},P_{e,2}\)per-hop errors
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Charlie receives, guesses the bit, retransmits. The bit is wrong if exactly one of the two hops errs.

State constellations and receiver types

Overlap between two states on a circle
\[ |\langle\alpha_i|\alpha_j\rangle|^2=e^{-|\alpha_i-\alpha_j|^2},\quad |\alpha_i-\alpha_j|^2=\begin{cases}4\bar n&\text{opposite}\\2\bar n&\text{adjacent (QPSK)}\end{cases} \]
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
The farther apart two points are in phase space the easier to tell apart.

Data generation: averaging states over the channel

Building labels by Monte Carlo (slide, pp. 7 to 9)
\[ \beta=\sqrt I\,\psi+\eta,\quad \rho=\mathbb E\bigl[|\beta\rangle\langle\beta|\bigr]\approx\frac1N\sum_{k=1}^{N}|\beta_k\rangle\langle\beta_k| \]

Helstrom as semidefinite programming

Helstrom as an SDP (slide, p. 10)
\[ P_e=1-\max_{\{\Pi_0,\Pi_1\}}\bigl[p_0\operatorname{Tr}(\Pi_0\rho_0)+p_1\operatorname{Tr}(\Pi_1\rho_1)\bigr],\quad \Pi_i\succeq0,\ \Pi_0+\Pi_1=I \]
  • \(\Pi_i\)POVM elements
Multi-state SDP cost (main.pdf, p. 23)
\[ \text{complexity}\sim O(n_{\max}^3):\quad n_{\max}=10,20,30\ \Rightarrow\ 1000,\ 8000,\ 27000 \]

The slide reports "10 to 100 ms per solve at n_max = 20". The project figure 85.24 ms per sample comes from a separate experiment, not this slide.

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
The SDP finds the best measurement; for two states there is a compact eigenvalue solution, for many states it must be solved numerically.

The Cholesky head and validity by construction

The Cholesky head makes valid density matrices (slide, pp. 15 and 16)
\[ \rho_i=\frac{L_iL_i^{\dagger}}{\operatorname{Tr}(L_iL_i^{\dagger})},\qquad L_i\ \text{lower triangular},\ \ h\in\mathbb R^{256}\to L_i \]
  • \(\rho_i\succeq0\)positive semidefinite by construction
  • \(\operatorname{Tr}\rho_i=1\)unit trace

Example \(L=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\2&3\end{pmatrix}\): \(LL^{\top}=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&13\end{pmatrix}\), trace \(14\), \(\rho=\tfrac1{14}LL^{\top}\) has eigenvalues \(0.04825\) and \(0.9518\) (both positive, sum 1).

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Multiplying a matrix by its own transpose always gives a non-negative matrix; dividing by the trace makes it sum to 1.

The loss: states plus Helstrom consistency

Loss function (slide, p. 18)
\[ \mathcal L=\mathcal L_{\mathrm{state}}+\lambda\,\mathcal L_{\mathrm{Helstrom}},\quad \mathcal L_{\mathrm{state}}=\sum_{i=0}^{1}\|\rho_i^{\mathrm{pred}}-\rho_i^{\mathrm{ref}}\|_F,\quad \mathcal L_{\mathrm{Helstrom}}=(P_e^{\mathrm{pred}}-P_e^{\mathrm{ref}})^2 \]
PINN-style constraints in the 27-2 slides
\[ L_{\mathrm{herm}}=\|\rho-\rho^{\dagger}\|_F^2,\quad L_{\mathrm{trace}}=(\operatorname{Tr}\rho-1)^2,\quad L_{\mathrm{pos}}=\sum_k\max(0,-\lambda_k)^2 \]

The Cholesky head makes these three terms vanish by construction, so no penalty is needed; this is why the later version drops them.

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
The network is penalised when predicted states differ from the reference and when the P_e computed from them differs from the reference P_e.

Worked number: shot noise dominates the heterodyne receiver

Receiver noise variance (Eq. 15) with Table 3
\[ \sigma_n^2=2q\bar g^{2+x}\bigl[\Re(P_R+P_{LO})+I_d\bigr]\Delta f+\frac{4k_BT}{R_L}\Delta f \]

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners

Worked number: the Hermiticity, trace and positivity penalties

Three constraint penalties for a badly predicted matrix
\[ \rho=\begin{pmatrix}.7&.1\\.2&.4\end{pmatrix}:\ \ L_{\mathrm{herm}}=0.02,\quad L_{\mathrm{trace}}=0.01,\quad L_{\mathrm{pos}}=0 \]

The matrix is not symmetric (0.1 versus 0.2) and has trace \(1.1\); its symmetrised part has eigenvalues \(0.3379\) and \(0.7621\), so the positivity penalty is 0.

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Each penalty measures one kind of fault: asymmetry, trace not 1, or a negative eigenvalue.

Satellite QKD with key retransmission

QBER and sift probability (Eqs. 17 to 19)
\[ \mathrm{QBER}=\frac{P_{\mathrm{error}}}{P_{\mathrm{sift}}},\quad P_{\mathrm{error}}=P_{A,B}(0,1)+P_{A,B}(1,0),\quad P_{\mathrm{sift}}=\sum_{a,b}P_{A,B}(a,b) \]
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
This system belongs to the same free-space-optics family as the project, but the problem is the secret key rather than the Helstrom bound.

Dual-threshold decisions: trading errors for key rate

The dual-threshold decision rule (Eq. 16)
\[ \hat b=\begin{cases}0&i\ge d_0\\1&i\le d_1\\X&\text{otherwise}\end{cases},\qquad \mathrm{QBER}\le10^{-3},\ P_{\mathrm{sift}}\ge10^{-2} \]
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
A wider "X" zone yields fewer wrong bits but discards more bits.

QPSK bases, the X bit and the QBER calculation

Fraction of bits surviving the sift
\[ \Pr[\text{same basis}]=\tfrac12,\qquad \text{example (assumed): }\ \mathrm{QBER}=\frac{2\times10^{-4}}{0.1}=0.002\ \gt10^{-3} \]
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Alice and Bob pick bases at random; only about half the time they match, the rest become "X" and are dropped.

From the Helstrom bound to a laboratory receiver

BPSK error: Helstrom versus Kennedy
\[ P_{\mathrm{Hel}}=\tfrac12\bigl(1-\sqrt{1-e^{-4\bar n}}\bigr),\qquad P_{\mathrm{Ken}}=\tfrac12e^{-4\bar n}\ \ (\text{extra, module 09}) \]
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
The Helstrom bound is the theoretical best; a feedback receiver with an FPGA as in the figure is a way to approach it.

Physics-guided ML and the two lab approaches

Physics-guided machine learning (Karpatne, 2018)
\[ \mathcal L=\mathcal L_{\mathrm{data}}+\lambda_1\mathcal L_{\mathrm{boundary}}+\lambda_2\mathcal L_{\mathrm{mono}}+\lambda_3\mathcal L_{\mathrm{smooth}}\quad(\text{slide 27-2, Approach 2}) \]
  • Theory-based models have knowledge gaps; data models need many samples; combining both is "theory-guided data science".
  • The 27-2 slides use the idea in two ways: constraint penalties (Approach 1) and boundary and monotonic regularisers for P_e (Approach 2).
  • Boundary: \(P_e\to0.5\) when the signal vanishes, consistent with \(\tfrac12(1-\sqrt{1-e^{-4\bar n}})\) as \(\bar n\to0\).

\[ R=\log_2M:\quad M=2\to1,\quad M=3\to1.585,\quad M=4\to2\ \ \text{bits/symbol} \]

A real case: the standard SDP frame for many states

Case thật · Mironowicz (2024): state discrimination as an SDP

Appendix B.3 of the 94-page review describes discriminating N non-orthogonal states: choose measurement operators \(M_i\) to maximise the average success probability, with a YALMIP example of three 3-dimensional states.

\[ P_{\mathrm{succ}}=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_iM_i),\quad M_i\succeq0,\ \ \sum_iM_i=I,\qquad P_e=1-P_{\mathrm{succ}} \]
Mironowicz, J. Phys. A 57, 163002, 2024, Appendix B.3, pp. 73 and 74.
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
For two states this problem has a closed form via eigenvalues; for many states an SDP solver is required.

  • Reading two duplicates as two independent sources.
  • Treating the 4-page thesis excerpt as the full results.
