The Helstrom problem as an SDP

Helstrom as a semidefinite program (SDP)
\[ \max_{\{\Pi_i\}}\ \sum_{i=0}^{M-1} p_i\,\mathrm{Tr}(\rho_i\Pi_i)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad \Pi_i\succeq 0,\ \ \sum_i\Pi_i=I_d \]
  • \(\rho_i\)received state for symbol \(i\)
  • \(\Pi_i\)POVM element, a \(d\times d\) matrix
  • \(p_i\)prior probability

The largest success probability is \(1-P_e^{\min}\). With \(M\) hypotheses and a Fock space truncated at \(d\), the problem has \(Md^2\) real variables.

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Finding the best measurement is a constrained optimisation. A general solver (SDP) handles any \(M\) but is slow.

Standard form of a semidefinite program

Standard form (1)
\[ \min_x\ c^{T}x\quad\text{s.t.}\quad F(x)=F_0+\sum_{i=1}^{m}x_iF_i\succeq 0 \]
  • \(F_i\)\(m+1\) symmetric \(n\times n\) matrices
  • \(m,n\)number of variables, matrix size

The feasible set is convex with a piecewise smooth boundary, and the optimum lies on the boundary (Vandenberghe and Boyd, p. 50).

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
The "matrix is non-negative" constraint is curved and non-smooth, yet convex. Convexity is why reliable algorithms exist.

Problem size versus M and d

With \(M\) states and matrices of size \(d\), the SDP has \(m=Md^2\) variables and constraint size \(n=d\).

Per-iteration cost in \(M,d\)
\[ T_{\text{iter}}=O(m^2n^2)=O\bigl((Md^2)^2\,d^2\bigr)=O(M^2d^6) \]
  • \(m=Md^2\)variables
  • \(n=d\)PSD matrix size

Barrier function and central path

Log-det barrier
\[ \phi(x)=\log\det F(x)^{-1},\qquad x^\star(\gamma)=\arg\min_{c^Tx=\gamma}\phi(x) \]
  • \(\phi\)finite inside, \(\to\infty\) at the boundary
  • \(\gamma\)objective level

Interior-point methods follow this path from the center to the optimum without touching the boundary.

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Picture walking from the middle of a room to the door without brushing the walls. The barrier is a soft wall that pushes you away from the boundary.

Iteration count of interior-point methods

Iteration count
\[ k_{\text{worst}}=O\bigl(\sqrt{n}\,\log(1/\epsilon)\bigr),\qquad k_{\text{practice}}\approx 5\text{ to }50 \]
  • \(\epsilon\)relative accuracy

In practice the count grows slower than \(\sqrt n\), perhaps like \(\log n\) or \(n^{1/4}\) (p. 84).

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Theory gives the worst case, while practice needs a few dozen rounds. The real cost sits inside each round.

Per-iteration cost O(m²n²)

Per-iteration and total cost
\[ T_{\text{iter}}=O(m^2n^2),\qquad T_{\text{SDP}}\approx k\,T_{\text{iter}}\ \ (k\approx 20) \]
  • \(m^2n^2\)least squares with \(m\) variables and \(n(n+1)/2\) equations (Sec. 7.6, p. 89)
  • \(k\)typical iteration count

Eigendecomposition in place of the SDP

Two-hypothesis Helstrom bound via eigenvalues
\[ P_e^{\min}=\tfrac12\Bigl(1-\bigl\|\tfrac12(\rho_1-\rho_0)\bigr\|_1\Bigr)=\tfrac12\Bigl(1-\tfrac12\sum_{k=1}^{d}|\lambda_k|\Bigr) \]
  • \(\lambda_k\)eigenvalues of \(\rho_1-\rho_0\) (real symmetric)
  • \(\|\cdot\|_1\)trace norm

For \(M=2\) with equal priors no SDP is needed: only the \(d\) eigenvalues of a \(d\times d\) matrix.

Cross-checkOn 20 samples the SDP (CVXPY/SCS) and the eigendecomposition differ by at most \(2.08\times10^{-6}\), median \(2.63\times10^{-7}\) (EXPERIMENTS.md, entry of 5 Oct 2026).
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
With two symbols the answer is packed into the eigenvalues of the difference of two density matrices. All that remains is diagonalisation.

The quadrature oracle of the project

Channel-averaged state by quadrature
\[ \rho_{0,1}=\mathbb E_{I,w}\bigl[\,|\beta\rangle\langle\beta|\,\bigr]\approx\sum_{i=1}^{n_I}\sum_{j=1}^{n_w}u_iv_j\,|\beta_{ij}^{\pm}\rangle\langle\beta_{ij}^{\pm}|,\quad \beta_{ij}^{\pm}=\pm\sqrt{I_i}\sqrt{\bar n}+\sigma w_j \]
  • \(u_i,I_i\)Gauss-Legendre weights and nodes in \(\log I\) (Gamma-Gamma density)
  • \(v_j,w_j\)Gauss-Hermite weights and nodes for the noise
  • \(K=n_In_w\)total quadrature nodes

This is exactly the label pipeline of the project (ttn-12-08, quadrature_baseline.py): deterministic, no Monte Carlo noise.

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Instead of drawing millions of random samples, we sum with weights over a few hundred to a few thousand cleverly chosen points.

  • \(c\,d^3\)eigendecomposition, \(c=10\) (assumption)

Eigenvalues by accelerated gradient descent

Iteration count versus spectral gap \(\delta\)
\[ \tilde O\bigl(1/\sqrt{\delta}\bigr)\ \text{vs}\ \tilde O(1/\delta) \]
  • \(\delta=\lambda_p-\lambda_{p+1}\)spectral gap

Iterative methods target the first \(p\) eigenpairs of a huge matrix. A Helstrom problem with \(d\le 64\) needs the entire spectrum, so the direct \(O(d^3)\) route stays sensible.

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
For gigantic matrices one iterates to get a few leading eigenvalues. For a \(16\times16\) matrix direct diagonalisation is quicker.

D-decomposition and its limits of use

D-decomposition and its claimed cost
\[ A=PDQ,\qquad O(n^{3})\ \longrightarrow\ O(n^{2}k)\ \ (k\ll n) \]
  • \(P,D,Q\)three structured factors (sparse, low rank)
  • \(k\)structure parameter, e.g. rank
Why it does not applyKatende (arXiv:2409.06321, pp. 2 and 3, Corollary 1) claims \(O(n^2k)\) only for sparse or low-rank \(A\). The \(16\times16\) matrix \(\rho_1-\rho_0\) is dense and full rank, and the paper does not treat eigenvalues. Use it only as a reminder that \(O(n^3)\) is the standard cost of LU, QR and Cholesky.

Single author, arXiv v1, not peer reviewed; the archive notes mislabel the author as "Zhao et al.".

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
The method is fast for sparse or low-rank matrices. Ours is small and dense, so it gains nothing.

Break-even from the same-session timings

Break-even point
\[ N^{*}=\frac{T_{\text{up-front}}}{t_{\text{ref}}-t_{\text{sur}}} \]
  • \(T_{\text{up-front}}\)training plus label generation, paid up front
  • \(t_{\text{ref}}-t_{\text{sur}}\)time saved per evaluation

One seed, 2000 epochs, \(T=14\,687\) s (EXPERIMENTS.md, fit_sec of seed 2024):

\[ N^{*}_{\text{SDP}}=\frac{14\,687}{0.1139-0.0000209}\approx1.3\times10^{5},\qquad N^{*}_{\text{oracle}}=\frac{14\,687}{0.002718-0.0000209}\approx5.4\times10^{6} \]

Both results match the figures stated in EXPERIMENTS.md (\(1.3\times10^5\) and \(5.4\times10^6\)).

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Training is like buying a machine: a big cost up front, then each use is cheap. You must use it often enough to profit.

FLOPs and latency are different measures

Latency is not just FLOPs
\[ t\approx\max\Bigl(\frac{F}{P_{\text{peak}}},\ \frac{B}{\mathrm{BW}}\Bigr)+t_0 \]
  • \(F,B\)FLOPs and bytes moved
  • \(t_0\)fixed call overhead

The DNN literature stresses that moving data often costs more than computing.

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Two networks with the same operation count can run at very different speeds depending on how data reaches memory and how functions are called.

Per-call versus batched evaluation

Per call versus batched
\[ t_{\text{call}}=t_0+t_{\text{compute}},\qquad t_{\text{batch}}=\frac{t_0}{B}+t_{\text{compute}} \]
  • \(B\)batch size (256 in the measurement)
  • \(t_0\)call overhead, about \(0.8\) ms
VersusBatchedPer call
SDP (114 ms)6356×144×
Oracle 48×41 (13.5 ms)757×17×
Citation trapA parameter sweep that calls the network point by point is only 17 times faster than the oracle (or 3 times with the 24×7 grid), not 757 times. The large gain needs batched evaluation.
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Every function call has a fixed fee. Packing 256 samples into one call splits the fee 256 ways.

Network pruning by the Taylor criterion

Taylor criterion
\[ \Theta_{TE}(h_i)=\Bigl|\frac{\partial\mathcal C}{\partial h_i}\,h_i\Bigr|\quad(\text{Eq. 7}) \]
  • \(h_i\)output of a parameter or feature map
  • \(\mathcal C\)cost function

Remove the unit with the smallest estimated cost change, then fine-tune by backpropagation (Sec. 2.2, p. 4).

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Drop the neurons whose removal barely changes the result, then lightly retrain.

FLOPs-driven pruning

FLOPs regularisation
\[ \Theta(z_l^{(k)})=\Theta(z_l^{(k)})-\lambda\,\Theta_{\text{flops}}\quad(\text{Eq. 9}),\ \ \lambda=10^{-3} \]
  • \(\Theta_{\text{flops}}\)FLOPs of the feature map (sliding window)

For our MLP, halving every width takes FLOPs from 498,432 to 194,176 (2.6 times), but nobody has checked the accuracy (illustration).

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Pruning can cut a large vision network by 12 times in FLOPs. A small network like the surrogate gains less because call overhead dominates its latency.

Quantisation and bit operations

BOP of a dense layer
\[ \mathrm{BOP}_{\text{Dense}}\approx n_nn_i\bigl[b_wb_i+b_w+b_i+\lceil\log_2 n_i\rceil\bigr]\quad(\text{Eq. 7}) \]
  • \(b_w,b_i\)bit width of weights and inputs

With \(b_w=b_i=8\), \(n_i=256\): the product term \(b_wb_i=64\) dominates the accumulator \(8+8+8=24\) bits (by Eq. 7).

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Using fewer bits per number makes the arithmetic circuit smaller and cheaper. This matters for hardware and little for a CPU run.

Limits of the break-even analysis

Limits of the cost analysis
\[ C_{\text{sur}}(N)=T+N\,t_{\text{sur}},\qquad C_{\text{ref}}(N)=N\,t_{\text{ref}} \]
  • \(T\)up-front cost, including labels and trial runs
  • \(N\)number of evaluations
  • The two \(T\) values differ 7.5-fold (14,687 s for 2000 epochs; 1.1×10⁵ s for 6000 epochs) because of different epoch counts and machine load.
  • \(T\) excludes ablation runs (many trainings), so the true break-even is further away.
  • The network is valid only inside the trained domain (\(\bar n\le1.1\), 3 noise levels); outside it \(T\) must be paid again.
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Break-even holds only if the reuse falls inside the region the network has learned.

A real case: counting equaliser complexity

Case thật · Freire et al.: a standard method to count network complexity

The journal paper proposes metrics that bridge software and hardware (RM, BOP, NABS) for dense, convolutional and recurrent layers, then applies them to optical equalisers: a biLSTM+CNN teacher and a 1D-CNN student.

A repeatable timing protocol

A repeatable measurement protocol
\[ \tilde t=\operatorname{median}(t_1,\dots,t_R),\qquad S=\tilde t_{\text{ref}}/\tilde t_{\text{sur}} \]
  • \(R\)repetitions (300 samples, 5 passes over test in the measurement)
  • \(\tilde t\)median, robust to a few slow calls
  • Same session, machine and thread count (Xeon 2.2 GHz, 4 cores, PyTorch 2 threads).
  • Report both mean and median: the FP64 closed form has mean 0.0532 ms but median 0.0058 ms.
  • Time batched and per-call separately; state the batch size.
  • Cross-check results across methods before comparing times (SDP versus eigendecomposition differ by at most \(2.08\times10^{-6}\)).
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
A fair measurement means equal conditions, many repetitions, and a clear statement of how the function was called.

Conclusions on the cost of computing the Helstrom bound

Main message
\[ T+N\,t_{\text{sur}}\ \lessgtr\ N\,t_{\text{ref}}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ N\ \lessgtr\ N^{*} \]
  • \(T\)training and labelling cost
  • The SDP is general but costs \(O(M^2d^6)\) per round, measured at 114 ms per sample.
  • For \(M=2\) the \(O(d^3)\) eigendecomposition gives the same answer; the quadrature oracle also packs the channel average (13.5 ms).
  • The surrogate takes 0.018 ms batched, about 757 times faster than the oracle, but breaks even only after about \(8\times10^6\) evaluations.
  • FLOPs give only an upper bound on the gain; latency depends on batching and call overhead.
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Pick a tool by how many times you will use it, not just by its one-off speed.

Detailed notes

In plain words

Computing the Helstrom bound is like marking exams: there is a general but very slow way (the SDP), a fast way when there are only two answers (eigenvalues), and a way of memorising answers beforehand (the surrogate). Memorising costs effort at first, so it pays off only when you must mark very many papers.

Theoretical and historical background

Vandenberghe and Boyd (1996) introduced interior-point methods for SDP: \(O(\sqrt n)\) iterations in theory, 5 to 50 in practice, each \(O(m^2n^2)\). Jiang et al. (FOCS 2020) reached \(O(\sqrt n(mn^2+m^\omega+n^\omega))\); Huang et al. (2021) brought it to \(m^\omega\) for \(m=\Omega(n^2)\) with a robust interior-point analysis. These are asymptotic results; at \(d=16\) a real solver uses warm starts, so measurements are far below the theoretical count. On the network side, Sze et al. (2017) stress data movement, Molchanov et al. (2017) prune by FLOPs, and Freire et al. (2022) standardise counting.

Worked examples

Ví dụ · FLOPs after halving the widths (digital engineering)
  1. Original trunk: \(2(9\cdot128+128\cdot128+128\cdot256+256\cdot256)=231\,680\).
  2. New trunk (64,64,128,128): \(2(9\cdot64+64\cdot64+64\cdot128+128\cdot128)=58,496\).
  3. Two heads at \(d=16\): \(4\cdot128\cdot256=131,072\), plus \(512+4096\).
  4. Total 194,176 versus 498,432: a 2.57-fold reduction. (illustration, accuracy not checked)
  5. But with per-call use the overhead \(t_0\approx0.8\) ms still takes nearly all the time, so latency barely changes.
Ví dụ · SDP cost for \(M=3,d=20\) (optimisation)
  1. \(m=Md^2=3\cdot400=1200\), \(n=20\).
  2. \(m^2n^2=1200^2\cdot400=576,000,000\).
  3. 20 rounds: 11,520,000,000 (matches the archive table: 11,520,000,000).
  4. Surrogate at the same \(d=20\): 650,080 FLOPs, ratio about 17721.
Ví dụ · FSO link-design sweep (assumption)
  1. A grid \(50\times3\times30\times20=90,000\) points (\(\bar n,\sigma,L,C_n^2\)). (assumption)
  2. Oracle: \(90,000\times13.5\) ms \(\approx 1215\) s (about 20 minutes).
  3. SDP: \(90,000\times114\) ms \(\approx 10260\) s (about 2.9 hours).
  4. Batched surrogate: \(90,000\times0.018\) ms \(\approx 1.6\) s, but training costs \(1.1\times10^5\) s.
  5. Conclusion: a single sweep needs only the oracle; the surrogate is worthwhile only after about \(10^2\) such sweeps (\(N^*\approx8\times10^6\)).
Ví dụ · Neural equaliser in optical communication (assumption)
  1. MLP 41 → 64 → 32 → 2, excluding the output layer as Freire et al. do. (assumption)
  2. \(\mathrm{RM}=41\cdot64+64\cdot32=4,672\) multiplications per symbol.
  3. At 64 GBd: \(4,672\times6.4\times10^{10}\approx 2.99e+14\) multiplications per second.
  4. Real-time constraints make RM and BOP matter here, unlike the offline surrogate.
Ví dụ · Scaling to \(d=64\) (scientific computing)
  1. Eigendecomposition: \(10\cdot64^3=2,621,440\).
  2. One SDP round, \(M=2\): \(m=8192\), \(m^2n^2=2.75e+11\).
  3. Surrogate: 4,696,320 FLOPs, of which the two heads take 89%.
  4. The \(d^6\) versus \(d^2\) orders show the gap swelling with \(d\), so large-\(d\) oracles are where a surrogate helps most.
Ví dụ · Break-even with the two measurement sets (verification)
  1. Session of 5 Oct: \(T=14\,687\) s, SDP 0.1139 s, oracle 2.718 ms, surrogate 0.0209 ms.
  2. \(N^*_{\text{SDP}}=1.29e+05\), \(N^*_{\text{oracle}}=5.45e+06\).
  3. Paper: \(T=1.1\times10^5\) s, oracle 13.5 ms, surrogate 0.018 ms: \(N^*=8.16e+06\).

In the project paper the "Cost per evaluation" table lists SDP 114 ms, oracle 13.5 ms, FP64 closed form 0.053 ms, log closed form 13.4 ms, lookup table \(1.5\times10^{-4}\) ms, surrogate 0.018 / 0.79 ms. Pitfalls in exercises

  • Writing \(m=M\cdot d\) instead of \(m=Md^2\).
  • Forgetting the factor 2 when converting RM to FLOPs.
  • Using \(t_{\text{ref}}\) and \(t_{\text{sur}}\) from different sessions.
  • Mixing \(T\) of 2000 epochs with a 6000-epoch claim.
  • Dividing by \(t_{\text{ref}}\) instead of \(t_{\text{ref}}-t_{\text{sur}}\).
