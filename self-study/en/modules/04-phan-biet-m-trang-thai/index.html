The M-ary PSK coherent-state discrimination problem

The M-PSK alphabet
\[ |\alpha_k\rangle,\quad \alpha_k=\sqrt{\bar n}\,e^{2\pi i k/M},\quad p_k=\tfrac1M,\quad k=0,\dots,M-1 \]
  • \(\bar n=|\alpha|^2\)mean photon number per pulse
  • \(M\)number of states; carries \(\log_2 M\) bits per symbol
Hypothesis-testing error
\[ P_{\rm err}=\sum_{m\neq l}P(H_m|H_l)\,P(H_l)=1-\tfrac1M\sum_{m}P(H_m|H_m) \]

Müller et al. use QPSK \(\alpha_n=|\alpha|e^{i(n-\frac12)\pi/2}\) with prior \(p=1/4\); BPSK is \(M=2\), QPSK is \(M=4\).

Source: Müller et al., New J. Phys. 14, 083009 (2012), Eq. (1), tr. 4.

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
The sender picks one of M phases and the receiver must guess which. Coherent light always carries quantum noise, so the guess can never be perfect.

Overlap and Gram matrix of the PSK alphabet

Inner product of two coherent states
\[ \langle\alpha|\beta\rangle=\exp\!\Bigl(-\tfrac{|\alpha|^2}{2}-\tfrac{|\beta|^2}{2}+\alpha^{*}\beta\Bigr) \]
Overlap of two PSK states
\[ |\langle\alpha_i|\alpha_j\rangle|^2=\exp\!\Bigl[-2\bar n\bigl(1-\cos\tfrac{2\pi(i-j)}{M}\bigr)\Bigr] \]
  • QPSK adjacent\(e^{-2\bar n}\)

The Helstrom bound for QPSK

Helstrom bound for a geometrically uniform set
\[ P_{\rm c}^{\rm H}=\frac1{M^2}\Bigl(\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}\sqrt{\lambda_k}\Bigr)^{2},\qquad \lambda_k=\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}\langle\alpha_0|\alpha_j\rangle e^{-2\pi i jk/M} \]
  • \(\lambda_k\)eigenvalues of the (circulant) Gram matrix
  • \(P_{\rm err}^{\rm H}=1-P_{\rm c}^{\rm H}\)smallest error over all measurements

Many-photon asymptote: \(P^{\rm H}_{\rm err}\simeq\tfrac12e^{-2|\alpha|^2}\) for \(|\alpha|^2\gg1\).

For a rotationally symmetric set, the best possible limit follows from a few numbers (eigenvalues). The table shows the approximation is very close once the photon number is large.

The standard quantum limit of conventional receivers

Standard quantum limit (SQL) for QPSK
\[ P_{\rm SQL}=1-\Bigl[1-\tfrac12\,\mathrm{erfc}\bigl(\sqrt{|\alpha|^2/2}\bigr)\Bigr]^{2},\qquad \mathrm{erfc}(x)=\tfrac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\int_x^\infty e^{-t^2}\,dt \]
  • \(P_{\rm SQL}\)smallest error of an ideal 100%-efficient conventional receiver
  • \(\tfrac12\mathrm{erfc}(\cdot)\)error of a single quadrature

Two independent quadratures are measured at once (heterodyne, or dual homodyne), so the success probability is the product of two single-quadrature successes.

One homodyne quadrature
\[ P^{\rm HD}_{\rm err}=\tfrac12\bigl(1-\mathrm{erf}(r|\alpha|)\bigr) \]
  • \(r^2\)fraction of light entering the homodyne

Source: Becerra et al., Nature Photon. 7, 147 (2013), Eq. (2), tr. 148; Müller et al., New J. Phys. 14, 083009 (2012), Eq. (4), tr. 5.

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
The SQL is the best error of the classical way of measuring (read amplitude or phase, then decide). Quantum mechanics allows lower error than this.

The adaptive receiver: displacement and photon counting

Bayesian update
\[ P_{\rm post}(\psi|\beta_j,d_j)=A\,\mathbb P(d_j|\beta_j,\psi)\,P_{\rm prior}(\psi) \]
  • \(\beta_j\)hypothesis tested at step \(j\)
  • \(d_j\)photon count observed
  • \(A\)normalisation

If \(\beta_j\) is right, the field is displaced to vacuum and \(d_j=0\) for sure. The final decision is the state with the largest posterior after \(N\) steps.

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
The receiver guesses a state, subtracts it from the signal and counts photons. If the guess is right, no photon remains. Each count updates the guess.

Experimental realisation of the adaptive receiver

Total receiver efficiency
\[ \begin{aligned}\mathrm{DE}_{\rm Tot}&=T_1\,\mathrm{DE}_{\rm SPD}\,T_2\,(1-\text{loss})\\&\approx0.925\cdot0.840\cdot0.970\cdot0.9595=0.723\end{aligned} \]
  • 633 nm laser, \(N=10\) periods of \(\approx2.7\,\mu\)s, 10 MHz feedback bandwidth
  • interference visibility \(V=99.7\%\); single-photon detector \(84.0\%\)
  • each point: \(4\times10^{6}\) trials
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Two phase modulators: one prepares the state, one makes the displacement field. An FPGA processes counts in real time to choose the next displacement.

Result: error below the SQL for four states

Gain over the SQL in dB
\[ \begin{aligned}G&=10\log_{10}\frac{P_{\rm SQL}}{P_e}\\ G=6\ \text{dB}&\iff P_e=P_{\rm SQL}/3.98\end{aligned} \]
  • below the SQL for \(2\le\bar n\le15\), no efficiency correction
  • more than 6 dB for \(6\le\bar n\le11\): error about a quarter of the SQL
  • curve bends upward at large \(\bar n\) because visibility is below 100%
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
The dB scale measures "how many times better". 6 dB means the error is about a quarter of the conventional limit.

The hybrid receiver: a continuous then a discrete measurement

Beam splitting
\[ \begin{aligned}T&=t^2,\quad R=r^2=1-t^2\\ &\text{HD: } r\alpha,\quad \text{click stage: } t\alpha\end{aligned} \]
  • step 1: homodyne on the \(P\) quadrature discards half the states
  • step 2: a conditional displacement and a click detector choose between the two remaining
  • \(t^2\) is optimised to minimise the error
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Split the signal in two. The first part (homodyne) tells which half-plane it is in. The second part (click detector) separates the two remaining candidates.

The homodyne stage halves the phase plane

Homodyne POVM
\[ \hat\Pi^{\rm HD}_{+}=\int_0^\infty dp\,|p\rangle\langle p|,\qquad \hat\Pi^{\rm HD}_{-}=\mathbb 1-\hat\Pi^{\rm HD}_{+} \]
Error of the homodyne stage
\[ P^{\rm HD}_{\rm err}=\tfrac12\Bigl(1-\mathrm{erf}\bigl[\sqrt2\,\bigl(r|\alpha|/\sqrt2\bigr)\bigr]\Bigr)=\tfrac12\bigl(1-\mathrm{erf}(r|\alpha|)\bigr) \]
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
The \(P\)-quadrature measurement only reads a sign, so it rules out two of the four states. The effective amplitude is reduced by \(1/\sqrt2\).

\[ \hat\Pi^{\rm K}_{\rm no\,click}=|t\alpha_1\rangle\langle t\alpha_1|,\qquad P^{\rm K2}_{\rm err}=e^{-2|t\alpha|^2},\quad P^{\rm K}_{\rm err}=\tfrac12e^{-2|t\alpha|^2} \]
Mean error of HD-K
\[ P^{\rm HD\text{-}K}_{\rm err}=1-\tfrac12\bigl(1+\mathrm{erf}[r|\alpha|]\bigr)\Bigl(1-\tfrac12e^{-2|t\alpha|^2}\Bigr) \]

The optimised-displacement (OD) receiver for QPSK

Optimality condition for \(\beta\)
\[ \frac{t}{\sqrt2}\,\alpha=\beta\tanh\bigl(\sqrt2\,t\alpha\beta\bigr) \]
Error of the binary OD receiver
\[ P^{\rm OD}_{\rm err}=\tfrac12-\exp\!\Bigl(-t^2\tfrac{|\alpha|^2}{2}-|\beta|^2\Bigr)\sinh\bigl(\sqrt2\,t\alpha\beta\bigr) \]
Total displacement
\[ |\gamma|=\sqrt{\tfrac{|t\alpha|^2}{2}+|\beta|^2},\quad \varphi=\arctan\frac{|t\alpha|}{\sqrt2\,|\beta|} \]
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Instead of shifting exactly to vacuum like Kennedy, OD shifts a bit further to balance the two kinds of error. The direction must also rotate with signal power.

Weak limit: \(|\gamma|^2\to0.5\) and \(\varphi\to0\). Strong limit: \(|\gamma|^2\to|t\alpha|^2\) (as Kennedy) and \(\varphi\to\pi/4\). For \(|\alpha|^2\approx1\), \(t^2\approx0.5\) (\(\varphi=\arctan\) of Eq. (11)).

Optimal parameters versus signal power

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
For weak signals, split the light nearly evenly between the two stages. For strong signals, give most to the homodyne because click detectors suffer from dark counts.

Worked example 1: QPSK Helstrom at one photon

Step 1: Gram matrix and eigenvalues, \(\bar n=1\)
\[ \langle\alpha_0|\alpha_1\rangle=e^{-1+i}=0.1988+0.3096\,i,\qquad \lambda=(1.5329,\ 1.4838,\ 0.7378,\ 0.2455) \]
Step 2: Helstrom
\[ \sum_k\sqrt{\lambda_k}=1.2381+1.2181+0.8590+0.4955=3.8107,\quad P_{\rm c}=\tfrac{3.8107^2}{16}=0.9076,\quad P^{\rm H}_{\rm err}=0.0924 \]
Step 3: compare with the SQL
\[ P_{\rm SQL}=1-\bigl[1-\tfrac12\mathrm{erfc}(\sqrt{0.5})\bigr]^2=1-0.8413^2=0.2921 \]
  • ratio\(0.2921/0.0924=3.16\)

Computed by m04_verify.py; \(\sum\lambda_k=4=M\) is a consistency check (the Gram trace equals \(M\)).

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Build the overlap table, take eigenvalues, add square roots and square the sum. The result is the smallest possible error.

Worked example 2: reading the SQL, experiment and Helstrom gap

Reading the numbers at \(\bar n=10\)
\[ \begin{aligned}P_{\rm SQL}&=1.565\times10^{-3},\quad \tfrac{P_{\rm SQL}}{4}=3.91\times10^{-4}\\ P^{\rm H}&=1.03\times10^{-9}\end{aligned} \]

At \(\bar n=10\) the distance to Helstrom is still six orders of magnitude. The experiment captures only a small part of that gap.

\(10\log_{10}4=6.02\) dB.

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Three curves on one plot: SQL (red), experiment (blue dots) and Helstrom (black, lowest). 6 dB corresponds to about a factor of 4.

Worked example 3: hand computation of the HD-K error

HD-K at \(\bar n=1\), \(t^2=0.593\)
\[ r=\sqrt{1-0.593}=0.638,\quad P^{\rm HD}_{\rm err}=\tfrac12\bigl(1-\mathrm{erf}(0.638)\bigr)=0.1835,\quad P^{\rm K}_{\rm err}=\tfrac12e^{-2\cdot0.593}=0.1527 \]
Combine the two stages
\[ P^{\rm HD\text{-}K}_{\rm err}=1-(1-0.1835)(1-0.1527)=0.3082 \]

Heterodyne at \(\bar n=1\) gives \(0.2921\), so HD-K is worse by about \(5.5\%\). This matches the threshold Müller states: HD-K wins only for \(|\alpha|^2\gtrsim1.6\).

Homodyne windows: the trade-off between probability and fidelity

Window success probability and fidelity
\[ \begin{aligned}p_{w_0}&=\tfrac12\!\int_{\sqrt\gamma\alpha-\Delta}^{\infty}\!\!dx\,\bigl(|\psi_{\sqrt\gamma\alpha}|^2+|\psi_{-\sqrt\gamma\alpha}|^2\bigr)\\ |\psi_\beta(x)|^2&=\sqrt{\tfrac2\pi}e^{-2(x-\mathrm{Re}\beta)^2}\end{aligned} \]

\(\Delta=\Delta_0=\sqrt\gamma\alpha\) gives \(P_{\rm succ}=1\) but low fidelity; small \(\Delta\) gives high fidelity but low probability.

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Results between the two decision regions are discarded. The more we discard, the surer we are but the fewer successes we get.

The hybrid repeater: coherent light as a "bus" for qudits

Dispersive qudit-light interaction
\[ U_d(\theta)=e^{i\theta S_z^{(d)}a^\dagger a},\ \ \theta=\tfrac{2\pi}{d}:\quad \tfrac1{\sqrt d}\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}|k\rangle|\alpha\rangle\ \mapsto\ \tfrac1{\sqrt d}\sum_{k}|k\rangle\bigl|\alpha e^{2\pi i k/d}\bigr\rangle \]
  • \(S_z^{(d)}|k\rangle=\tfrac{2k-d+1}{2}|k\rangle\)generalised spin operator
  • \(L_{\rm att}\approx22\) kmattenuation length at telecom wavelength

Output state after loss: \(\rho_{\rm out}=\sum_{m}\tfrac{N_{v_m}(\sqrt{1-\gamma}\alpha)}{d^2}|T_m\rangle\langle T_m|\)

Entanglement negativity: \(\mathcal N(\rho)=\tfrac{\|\rho^{T_A}\|_1-1}{2}\)

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Atoms hold information for long, light carries it far. Each atomic level is tagged with a different light phase, so telling the phases apart reads out the atomic level.
Window limit
\[ \Delta\le\Delta_0=\tfrac12\sqrt{\tfrac34}\,\sqrt\gamma\,\alpha,\qquad \mathcal N_{\rm qubit\ Bell}=0.5 \]

Homodyne performance for qutrits

Average fidelity
\[ F_{\rm av}=\frac{\sum_{i=0}^2p_{w_i}F_{w_i}}{P_{\rm succ}} \]

With \(\Delta=0.2\Delta_0\), \(\alpha\approx1\): \(F_{\rm av}\approx0.7\) at \(P_{\rm succ}\approx0.4\) (p. 9). For large \(\alpha\), \(F_{\rm av}\to1/3\).

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
The two knobs \(\alpha\) and \(\Delta\) each have a sweet spot; you cannot maximise both probability and accuracy.

Connection to the group’s Helstrom surrogate

Helstrom from eigenvalues, no SDP for PSK
\[ P^{\rm H}_{\rm err}=1-\frac1{M^2}\Bigl(\sum_k\sqrt{\lambda_k}\Bigr)^2 \qquad\text{vs}\qquad P^{\rm H}_{\rm err}=\tfrac12\bigl(1-\|\tfrac12(\rho_1-\rho_0)\|_1\bigr) \]
  • A PSK set over a clean channel: closed form, a few eigenvalues.
  • After a Gamma-Gamma channel: \(\rho_k\) is a mixture, so density matrices and \(\mathrm{eigvalsh}\) or an SDP are needed; the group’s surrogate replaces this step.
  • Group figures: SDP 85.24 ms/sample, surrogate 0.118 ms/sample (CLAUDE.md, results_final).
Open directionHybrid and adaptive receivers for M states can serve as baselines between the SQL and Helstrom when evaluating the surrogate.
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
For a clean PSK set symmetry gives an exact answer. With turbulence the symmetry breaks and computation is needed, which is when a surrogate helps.

Detailed notes

Theory and historical background

For two states Helstrom (1967) gave a closed solution and Dolinar (1973) proposed a feedback receiver. For \(M\gt2\) no practical receiver reaches the Helstrom bound; Bondurant (1993) and Takeoka-Sasaki (2008) gave near-optimum receivers with displacement and photon counting. Becerra et al. (2013) gave the first experiment where four QPSK states beat the standard quantum limit (SQL) of the heterodyne receiver. Müller et al. (2012) proposed a hybrid homodyne-then-photon-counting receiver. Bergmann and van Loock (2017) bring coherent light into a repeater for qudits.

Plain words, technique, table, calculation

Plain words: four weak laser pulses differ by 90 degrees of phase; guess which one was sent. Technique: \(P_{\rm err}=1-\tfrac1{M^2}(\sum_k\sqrt{\lambda_k})^2\) with \(\lambda_k\) the eigenvalues of the circulant Gram matrix. Table: see the slide "Summary of QPSK receivers". Calculation: the examples below.

Example · QPSK at one photon (telecom)

\(\bar n=1\): Helstrom 0.09242, heterodyne (SQL) 0.2921. The ratio SQL/Helstrom is \(3.16\), so Helstrom is about 3.2 times lower.

Example · Quantum optics: BPSK versus QPSK at 0.5 photon

BPSK: Helstrom 0.03506, homodyne 0.07865. QPSK: Helstrom 0.2617. Carrying one more bit per symbol raises the error clearly (illustration at equal \(\bar n\)).

Example · Receiver design for optical communications (HD-K and HD-OD)

\(\bar n=1\): optimal HD-K \(t^2=0.593\) gives \(P_e=0.3082\); HD-OD \(t^2=0.503\) gives \(P_e=0.2721\); heterodyne 0.2921. Only HD-OD is below heterodyne.

Example · Long link at 10 photons (communications illustration)

\(\bar n=10\): SQL 0.00156, Helstrom 1.03e-09. Several orders of magnitude apart; Becerra et al. beat the SQL but remain far from Helstrom.

Example · Qutrit repeater (quantum networks, illustration)

5 km segment, \(\alpha=1.2\): \(F_0,F_1,F_2=0.7493, 0.09422, 0.1564\), sum \(=1\). The qutrit beats an ideal qubit at this distance (Bergmann-van Loock, 2017).

Case thật · How to read the error plot of Becerra et al.

Fig. 4 of Becerra et al. plots \(P_{\rm err}\) against \(\bar n\) with the SQL and Helstrom curves. At \(\bar n=10\) the SQL gives 0.00156; the measured points lie below the SQL and above the Helstrom value 1.03e-09.

Pitfalls in exercises

  • Mixing \(\bar n=|\alpha|^2\) and \(|\alpha|\) in the overlap.
  • Using the two-state formula for \(M=4\): the factors differ.
  • Forgetting the square in \(\tfrac1{M^2}(\sum\sqrt{\lambda_k})^2\).
  • Using the Kennedy \(t^2\) for OD: the two optima differ.
  • Trusting the SRM as optimal when the set is not symmetric.
