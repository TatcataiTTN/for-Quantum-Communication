From two-state BPSK to three-state modulation

Spectral efficiency
\[ \eta=\log_2 M \;\Rightarrow\; \eta_{\text{BPSK}}=1,\quad \eta_{3}=\log_2 3\approx 1.585 \]
  • \(M\)number of states in the alphabet
  • \(\eta\)bits carried per symbol

Gain over BPSK: \(\log_2 3-1\approx 58.5\%\). The price is a smaller spacing between states, from \(2\sqrt{\bar n}\) down to \(\sqrt{3\bar n}\).

📖 Plain-language explanation
Instead of sending one bit (two states) we send one of three states, so each symbol carries more information. Three states sit closer together than two antipodal ones, so they are easier to confuse.

The equilateral-triangle constellation of three coherent states

Three-state constellation
\[ \alpha_k=\sqrt{\bar n}\,\omega^{k},\quad \omega=e^{2\pi i/3},\ k=0,1,2 \]
  • \(\bar n=|\alpha_k|^2\)mean photon number per symbol
  • \(\omega\)cube root of unity

The centroid sits at the origin: \(\alpha_0+\alpha_1+\alpha_2=\sqrt{\bar n}(1+\omega+\omega^2)=0\). Every pair is \(120^\circ\) apart.

📖 Plain-language explanation
Put three points evenly on a circle: every state has the same energy and every pair is equally far apart, so no state is disadvantaged.

Pairwise distances of the three-state constellation

Pairwise distance
\[ |\alpha_i-\alpha_j|^2=2\bar n-2\bar n\cos120^\circ=3\bar n \]
Scheme\(d\)\(d^2\)\(|\langle\alpha_i|\alpha_j\rangle|^2\)
BPSK (two states)\(2\sqrt{\bar n}\)\(4\bar n\)\(e^{-4\bar n}\)

Distance relative to BPSK: \(\sqrt3/2\approx0.866\) (13.4% smaller); relative to QPSK: \(\sqrt{3/2}\approx1.225\).

📖 Plain-language explanation
The farther apart two points are, the easier they are to tell apart. Three states are farther apart than QPSK but closer than BPSK.

Coherent states and Poisson photon statistics

Fock expansion of a coherent state
\[ |\alpha\rangle=e^{-|\alpha|^2/2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}}\,|n\rangle \]
  • \(|n\rangle\)state with exactly \(n\) photons
  • \(\alpha\in\mathbb C\)complex amplitude: modulus is the amplitude, argument is the phase
Photon statistics
\[ P(n)=|\langle n|\alpha\rangle|^2=e^{-\bar n}\frac{\bar n^{\,n}}{n!},\qquad \mathbb E[n]=\operatorname{Var}(n)=\bar n=|\alpha|^2 \]

📖 Plain-language explanation
An ideal laser beam has no fixed photon number: it fluctuates following a Poisson law. The mean \(\bar n\) says how strong the beam is, the phase says what it encodes.

Overlap between two coherent states

Inner product of two coherent states
\[ \langle\alpha|\beta\rangle=\exp\!\Bigl(-\tfrac{|\alpha|^2}{2}-\tfrac{|\beta|^2}{2}+\alpha^{*}\beta\Bigr),\qquad |\langle\alpha|\beta\rangle|^2=e^{-|\alpha-\beta|^2} \]

For the three states: \(g=\langle\alpha_0|\alpha_1\rangle=e^{-\bar n(1-\omega)}=e^{-3\bar n/2}\,e^{\,i\sqrt3\,\bar n/2}\), so \(|g|=e^{-3\bar n/2}\) and \(|g|^2=e^{-3\bar n}\).

The Gram matrix of the three states

Gram matrix (circulant)
\[ G_{jk}=\langle\alpha_j|\alpha_k\rangle,\qquad G=\begin{pmatrix}1&g&\bar g\\ \bar g&1&g\\ g&\bar g&1\end{pmatrix} \]
  • \(g\)\(\langle\alpha_0|\alpha_1\rangle\), one complex number
  • \(\bar g\)complex conjugate of \(g\)

Example \(\bar n=1\): \(|g|=0.223\), \(\arg g=49.6^\circ\). The matrix depends only on \(k-j \bmod 3\), so its rows are cyclic shifts of each other.

Why it mattersFor pure states, everything about discrimination is contained in \(G\): changing the states while keeping \(G\) leaves \(P_e\) unchanged.
📖 Plain-language explanation
The Gram matrix is a table of the overlap of every pair. The three states are symmetric, so the table has a single independent value, \(g\).

Ring symmetry and the rotation operator in Fock space

Rotation operator
\[ U=e^{\,i\frac{2\pi}{3}\hat n},\qquad U|\alpha\rangle=|\omega\alpha\rangle,\qquad |\alpha_k\rangle=U^{k}|\alpha_0\rangle,\quad U^3=\mathbb 1 \]
  • \(\hat n=\hat a^\dagger\hat a\)photon-number operator
  • \(U^3=\mathbb 1\)because \(e^{2\pi i\hat n}=\mathbb 1\) for integer \(n\)
Consequence for the Gram matrix
\[ \langle\alpha_j|\alpha_k\rangle=\langle\alpha_0|U^{\,k-j}|\alpha_0\rangle \;\Rightarrow\; G \text{ circulant} \]

A set of this form is called geometrically uniform: a symmetry group generates every state from one reference state.

📖 Plain-language explanation
Rotating the phase by 120° leaves the problem unchanged and only renames the states. This symmetry is what lets us solve the problem with a short formula instead of numerical optimisation.

The Gamma-Gamma turbulence channel with complex additive noise

Received state
\[ |\beta_k(I,\epsilon)\rangle=\bigl|\sqrt{I}\,\alpha_k+\epsilon\bigr\rangle,\qquad \epsilon\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,\sigma^2) \]
  • \(I\)random intensity factor (turbulence)
  • \(\epsilon\)additive complex noise
Gamma-Gamma and Rytov
\[ f_I(I)=\frac{2(\alpha\beta)^{\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}}}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}I^{\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}-1}K_{\alpha-\beta}\!\bigl(2\sqrt{\alpha\beta I}\bigr),\quad \sigma_R^2=1.23\,C_n^2k^{7/6}L^{11/6} \]

\(\mathbb E[I]=1\), \(\operatorname{Var}(I)=\tfrac1\alpha+\tfrac1\beta+\tfrac1{\alpha\beta}\). The channel is identical to BPSK; only the constellation changes.

📖 Plain-language explanation
The atmosphere makes the beam intensity fluctuate randomly (factor \(I\)) and adds noise. The received state is still coherent, just scaled and shifted.

Optimal discrimination as a POVM problem

Helstrom problem for M states
\[ \small P_e^{\min}=\min_{\{\Pi_i\}}\Bigl\{1-\sum_{i}p_i\,\mathrm{Tr}(\Pi_i\rho_i)\Bigr\},\ \Pi_i\succeq0,\ \sum_i\Pi_i=\mathbb 1 \]
  • \(p_i=1/M\)equal priors
  • \(\Pi_i\)measurement element: outcome \(i\) means "symbol \(i\)"
📖 Plain-language explanation
We look for the measurement that guesses right most often. Every measurement is a set of non-negative matrices that add up to the identity.

Duality: a certificate of optimality

SDP duality (correct form)
\[ \min\ \mathrm{Tr}\,Y\ \ \text{s.t.}\ \ Y\succeq p_i\rho_i\ \ \forall i,\qquad P_e^{\min}=1-\mathrm{Tr}\,Y^{*} \]
  • \(Y\)Hermitian dual variable

Any feasible \(Y\) gives \(\mathrm{Tr}\,Y\ge P_c\), hence the lower bound \(P_e\ge1-\mathrm{Tr}\,Y\). The quoted figure writes \(\max\mathrm{Tr}Y\), \(Y\preceq\tfrac13\rho_i\) and \(1-3\mathrm{Tr}Y^*\); that inequality direction is reversed relative to the dual of maximising \(P_c\), so read it in the form above.

📖 Plain-language explanation
The dual problem gives a lower bound on the error. If some measurement reaches that bound we know for certain it is optimal.

The Holevo necessary and sufficient conditions

Holevo conditions
\[ Y=\sum_i p_i\Pi_i\rho_i=Y^\dagger,\qquad Y-p_j\rho_j\succeq0\ \ \forall j \]

Then \(\{\Pi_i\}\) is optimal and \(P_c=\mathrm{Tr}\,Y\). The next sections check this numerically: for the square-root measurement at \(\bar n=1\), the smallest eigenvalue of \(Y-\tfrac13\rho_j\) is \(-5\times10^{-17}\), i.e. zero up to rounding.

📖 Plain-language explanation
To test whether a measurement is optimal we build the matrix \(Y\) and check it never exceeds any \(\tfrac13\rho_j\), in the sense that the difference is a non-negative matrix.

The square-root measurement (PGM) for a pure-state set

Square-root measurement (PGM) for pure states
\[ |\mu_i\rangle=\sum_{j}\bigl(G^{-1/2}\bigr)_{ji}\,|\alpha_j\rangle,\qquad \Pi_i=|\mu_i\rangle\langle\mu_i|,\qquad \sum_i\Pi_i=\mathbb 1_{\mathrm{span}} \]
  • \(G^{-1/2}\)inverse square root of the Gram matrix
  • \(|\mu_i\rangle\)measurement vector, as close to \(|\alpha_i\rangle\) as possible while orthonormal
Probability of success
\[ P_c=\frac1M\sum_i|\langle\mu_i|\alpha_i\rangle|^2=\frac1M\sum_i\bigl|(G^{1/2})_{ii}\bigr|^2 \]

The three vectors \(|\mu_i\rangle\) are orthonormal (inner product \(\delta_{ij}\)), so measuring them is a true projective measurement and needs no extra space.

📖 Plain-language explanation
We "bend" the three slightly overlapping states into the three nearest mutually perpendicular directions, then measure along those.

The PGM P_e formula from Gram eigenvalues

Diagonalisation by the discrete Fourier transform
\[ \lambda_m=\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}\langle\alpha_0|\alpha_j\rangle\,\omega^{jm},\qquad (G^{1/2})_{ii}=\frac1M\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\sqrt{\lambda_m} \]
  • \(\lambda_m\)real non-negative eigenvalues of \(G\)
  • \(\omega=e^{2\pi i/M}\)\(M\)-th root of unity
Error of the ring-symmetric PGM
\[ P_e=1-\frac1{M^{2}}\Bigl(\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\sqrt{\lambda_m}\Bigr)^{2} \]

Check: \(\sum_m\lambda_m=\mathrm{Tr}\,G=M\). If \(g=0\) then \(\lambda_m=1\) and \(P_e=0\).

📖 Plain-language explanation
Because the Gram matrix "rotates cyclically", it is diagonalised by the Fourier transform, so three numbers \(\lambda_m\) replace a large-matrix computation.

Closed form of the three eigenvalues and optimality of the PGM

The three Gram eigenvalues for M = 3
\[ \lambda_m=1+2e^{-3\bar n/2}\cos\!\Bigl(\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\bar n+\tfrac{2\pi m}{3}\Bigr),\quad m=0,1,2 \]
  • \(e^{-3\bar n/2}\)modulus \(|g|\)
  • \(\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\bar n\)argument of \(g\), in radians
Optimality
\[ P_e^{\text{PGM}}=P_e^{\text{Helstrom}} \]

Valid for geometrically uniform states with equal priors.

Reason: \(Y\) commutes with \(U\), so \(Y-p\rho_j\succeq0\) for \(j=0\) implies it for every \(j\). At \(\bar n=1\): \(\lambda=(0.5610, 1.150, 1.289)\), sum \(=3\).

📖 Plain-language explanation
Since every state is a rotated copy of one reference state, the "symmetric" measurement is also the best one. That is why pure three-state discrimination needs no SDP.

Worked example: P_e of three states at n̄ = 1

  1. Overlap: \(g=e^{-1(1-\omega)}\), \(|g|=e^{-1.5}=0.223\), \(\arg g=\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}=0.866\) rad.
  2. Eigenvalues: \(\lambda_m=1+2|g|\cos(0.866+2\pi m/3)\) gives \((1.289, 0.5610, 1.150)\), sum \(=3.00\).
  3. Roots: \(\sqrt{\lambda_m}=(1.135, 0.7490, 1.072)\), sum \(=2.957\).
  4. \(P_c=\tfrac19(2.957)^2=0.9714\), so \(P_e=0.02864\).
BPSK at the same \(\bar n=1\)
\[ P_e^{\text{BPSK}}=\tfrac12\bigl(1-\sqrt{1-e^{-4}}\bigr)=4.600\times10^{-3},\qquad \frac{P_e^{(3)}}{P_e^{\text{BPSK}}}=6.23 \]
📖 Plain-language explanation
Only three things are needed: the overlap, three eigenvalues, then one squaring. No optimisation problem has to be solved.

\[ P_{e,\text{relay}}=P_{e,1}+(1-P_{e,1})P_{e,2}\approx P_{e,1}+P_{e,2} \]

📖 Plain-language explanation

The pairwise union bound and the mislabelled "Chernoff bound"

Pairwise union bound
\[ P_e\le(M-1)\,P_e^{\text{pair}},\qquad P_e^{\text{pair}}=\tfrac12\bigl(1-\sqrt{1-e^{-3\bar n}}\bigr) \]

The group notes call \(\tfrac12e^{-3\bar n/4}\) a "Chernoff lower bound", but at \(\bar n=2\) it equals \(0.112\), about \(90.9\) times the exact \(P_e\), so it cannot be a lower bound. The union bound is valid: \(2P^{\text{pair}}=1.24\times10^{-3}\).

📖 Plain-language explanation
The union bound adds pairwise errors so it is always above the true error. A formula above the true error cannot be called a lower bound.

Beyond the pure case: mixed states that keep the rotation symmetry

Covariant symmetry after the channel
\[ \rho_k=U^{k}\rho_0U^{-k},\qquad \Pi_k=U^{k}\Pi_0U^{-k},\qquad \sum_{k=0}^{2}U^{k}\Pi_0U^{-k}=\mathbb 1 \]
  • \(\rho_0\)state averaged over \(I\) and \(\epsilon\): mixed, no longer pure
Reduced SDP
\[ P_c=\max_{\Pi_0\succeq0}\ \mathrm{Tr}(\Pi_0\rho_0)\ \ \text{s.t.}\ \ \sum_kU^k\Pi_0U^{-k}=\mathbb 1 \]

Circularly symmetric complex noise and the intensity factor \(I\) both preserve rotation symmetry, so each \(\rho_k\) is still a rotated copy of \(\rho_0\). Variables drop from \(3N^2\) to \(N^2\). But for mixed states the square-root measurement is in general no longer optimal.

📖 Plain-language explanation
After the channel the states stop being pure but remain three rotated copies of each other. We only need to find one measurement element and rotate it twice.

Hidden assumptions and copy errors in the notes

  • Different assumptions: the \(P_e\) tables above are for the clean channel, with no turbulence and no additive noise (\(I=1,\epsilon=0\)). The group results for the Gamma-Gamma channel need the average \(\mathbb E_{I,\epsilon}\).
  • Steps 28-29 say "four states": a leftover from the QPSK text; for three states read three.
  • Unequal priors: the PGM with different \(p_i\) is no longer optimal; the SDP must be solved.
Conditions under which the PGM is not optimal
\[ p_i\ne\tfrac1M\ \ \text{or}\ \ \rho_i\ \text{mixed}\ \Rightarrow\ P_e^{\text{PGM}}\ge P_e^{\min} \]
TrapDo not mix numbers from the clean channel with numbers from the turbulent channel in one comparison.
📖 Plain-language explanation
Every result holds only under its own assumptions. State which channel, pure or mixed states, equal priors or not.

Detailed notes

Plain words first, then the technique

Picture three torches of equal brightness whose phases differ by \(120^\circ\). The receiver sees only faint blurred light and must guess which torch it was. Because quantum light has built-in noise, no two torches are ever completely distinct: the overlap \(|g|^2=e^{-3\bar n}\) is non-zero. The best measurement turns the three possibilities into three perpendicular directions and checks which one the light is nearest to.

Theory and historical background

Optimal discrimination of two states was founded by Helstrom (1976). For many states there is no general formula; Holevo (1973) gave necessary and sufficient conditions, and Hausladen and Wootters (1994) named the square-root measurement. Eldar and Forney (2001) showed the PGM is optimal for geometrically uniform state sets. Our group applies this to the three-state constellation through the Gamma-Gamma channel (82-step derivation, January 2026).

Worked examples, step by step

Example · 1. P_e of three states at n̄ = 1 (optical communication)
  1. Overlap amplitude \(|g|=e^{-3\bar n/2}=0.2231\).
  2. Three eigenvalues \((1.289, 0.5610, 1.150)\), sum \(3\).
  3. Sum of roots \(2.957\).
  4. \(P_e=1-(2.957)^2/9=0.02864\).
  5. Conclusion: about 2.9% of symbols are misjudged even with the optimal measurement.
Example · 2. Power budget for an FSO link (telecom engineering, illustrative)
  1. Target \(P_e=10^{-6}\): BPSK needs \(\bar n=3.11\), three states need \(\bar n=4.37\).
  2. 1 ns symbol at 1550 nm: \(\hbar\omega=1.28\times10^{-19}\) J, so \(P=\bar n\hbar\omega/T\).
  3. BPSK: \(P=3.98\times10^{-10}\) W; three states: \(P=5.61\times10^{-10}\) W.
  4. At the same power, three states carry \(1.585\) bits per symbol: rate up \(58.5\%\), power cost \(+1.48\) dB.
Example · 3. Three-class classification in machine learning (data science, illustrative)
  1. A kernel matrix \(K_{jk}=\exp(-\gamma|x_j-x_k|^2)\) for three evenly spaced centres on a circle is also circulant, like \(G\) (\(\gamma=1\) matches \(|g|^2=e^{-|\alpha_j-\alpha_k|^2}\)).
  2. With \(\bar n=1\): first row \((1;\ 0.0498;\ 0.0498)\) gives the squared overlaps.
  3. Its eigenvalues tell how well the three classes separate: eigenvalues near 1 mean nearly orthogonal classes.
Example · 4. Three-phase power (electrical engineering, illustrative)
  1. Three phases \(120^\circ\) apart: \(1+\omega+\omega^2=0\), the three voltages sum to zero, like the constellation centroid at the origin.
  2. The rotation \(U\) corresponds to a cyclic phase permutation.
  3. Hence the Gram matrix of three phases diagonalises with a 3-point Fourier transform, as in this module.
Example · 5. Fock cut-off for the SDP
  1. \(\bar n=2\): \(N=3\bar n+10=16\).
  2. Poisson tail \(P(n\ge16)=4.80\times10^{-10}\), far below \(10^{-3}\).
  3. SDP variables \(3N^2=768\); for \(N=25\): \(1875\).
Example · 6. Two relay hops
  1. Each hop \(P_e=1.23\times10^{-3}\) (\(\bar n=2\)).
  2. \(P_{e,\text{relay}}=2P-P^2=2.453\times10^{-3}\).
  3. Ratio of direct to per-hop \(\sigma_R^2\): \(2^{11/6}=3.564\).

Exam traps

Frequent mistakes
  • Confusing \(|g|\) with \(|g|^2\): \(P_e\) uses \(|g|^2=e^{-3\bar n}\).
  • Forgetting the \(M^2\) in \(P_c=\tfrac1{M^2}(\sum\sqrt{\lambda_m})^2\) (for \(M=3\) divide by \(9\), not \(3\)).
  • Using the union bound as the exact value: it is \(1.5\times\) too large for large \(\bar n\).
  • Applying PGM optimality to mixed states or unequal priors.

From plain words to the calculation

Plain: three directions that are close together. Technique: a circulant Gram matrix. Table: three eigenvalues. Calculation: \(P_e=1-\tfrac19(\sum\sqrt{\lambda_m})^2\).
