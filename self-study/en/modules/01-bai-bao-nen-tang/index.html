The EPR argument and the correlated photon pair

Correlated two-photon state
\[ |\Psi\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\bigl(a_+^\dagger b_+^\dagger+a_-^\dagger b_-^\dagger\bigr)|0\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\bigl(|1,0,1,0\rangle+|0,1,0,1\rangle\bigr) \]
  • \(a_\pm,b_\pm\)annihilation operators for the horizontal (+) and vertical (−) modes going to analyser 1 and 2
  • \(|n_1,n_2,n_3,n_4\rangle\)photon numbers in the four modes \(a_+,a_-,b_+,b_-\)
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Two photons born together always agree in polarisation. Measuring one lets you predict the other for sure, however far apart they are.

Coherent states and their overlap

A coherent state \(|\alpha\rangle\) (the state of a stabilised laser beam) has a Poisson photon-number distribution. Two coherent states are not orthogonal, so they cannot be told apart perfectly; this returns in the relay paper (part 5).

Fock-basis expansion and overlap
\[ |\alpha\rangle=e^{-|\alpha|^2/2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha^n}{\sqrt{n!}}|n\rangle,\qquad P(n)=\frac{|\alpha|^{2n}e^{-|\alpha|^2}}{n!},\qquad |\langle\beta|\alpha\rangle|^2=e^{-|\alpha-\beta|^2} \]
  • \(\bar n=|\alpha|^2\)mean photon number
  • \(|\langle\beta|\alpha\rangle|^2\)squared overlap, falling with the squared distance
State pair\(|\langle\beta|\alpha\rangle|^2\)
\(\alpha=0.5,\ \beta=-0.5\)0.3679
\(\alpha=1,\ \beta=-1\)0.0183
\(\alpha=1.5,\ \beta=-1.5\)0.000123
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Picture each coherent state as a dot on a plane. The closer two dots, the harder they are to tell apart; the overlap measures how hard.

The locality assumption in hidden-variable theory

Locality and hidden variable
\[ I_{1\pm}(\lambda,\theta_1,\theta_2)=I_{1\pm}(\lambda,\theta_1),\qquad E(\theta_1,\theta_2)=N^{-1}\!\int f(\lambda)\,S_1(\lambda,\theta_1)\,S_2(\lambda,\theta_2)\,d\lambda \]
  • \(\lambda\)hidden variable, known only through a distribution \(\rho(\lambda)\)
  • \(S_i=\dfrac{I_i^+-I_i^-}{I_i}\)response of analyser \(i\), bounded \(|S_i|\le 1\)
  • \(f=\rho I_1I_2\)weight, \(N=\int f\,d\lambda\) is the normaliser

The locality assumption (13.8) says the response of analyser 1 does not depend on the angle \(\theta_2\) set at analyser 2. Under it, the correlation factorises into a product of two single-argument functions.

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
A local hidden-variable theory says each side’s outcome was fixed at the source and neither side "tells" the other.

The CHSH inequality from locality

The CHSH inequality
\[ |B|\le 2,\qquad B=E(\theta_1,\theta_2)-E(\theta_1,\theta_2^{\prime})+E(\theta_1^{\prime},\theta_2)+E(\theta_1^{\prime},\theta_2^{\prime}) \]
  • \(E(\theta_1,\theta_2)\)correlation when the two analysers sit at angles \(\theta_1,\theta_2\)
  • \(2\)local bound (Eq. 13.19)
Ví dụ · Derivation sketch from |S|≤1
  1. Write \(E(\theta_1,\theta_2)-E(\theta_1,\theta_2^{\prime})\) as an integral of \(f S_1 S_2[1\pm S_1^{\prime} S_2^{\prime}]\) (Eq. 13.18).
  2. Use \(|S_i|\le1\) to bound the absolute value by \(2\pm[E(\theta_1^{\prime},\theta_2^{\prime})+E(\theta_1^{\prime},\theta_2)]\).
  3. Rearrange to get |B| ≤ 2.
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
CHSH is a "test": if the world runs on local hidden variables, the signed sum of four correlations can never exceed 2.

Quantum mechanics violates CHSH

Quantum prediction and the Bell function
\[ E(\theta_1,\theta_2)=\cos 2\psi,\quad \psi=\theta_1-\theta_2;\qquad B=3\cos 2\psi-\cos 6\psi \]
  • \(B(22.5^\circ)=2\sqrt2\)violates CHSH: \(2\sqrt2\approx2.828>2\)
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
With well-chosen angles, quantum mechanics gives 2.83, above 2. So the local hidden-variable assumptions cannot all hold.

The (n, 2, d) scenario and outcome vectors

Outcome vectors (d outcomes)
\[ \sum_{k=0}^{d-1}\mathbf v_k=\mathbf 0,\qquad \mathbf v_i\cdot\mathbf v_j=-\frac1N\ (i\ne j),\qquad N=d-1 \]
  • \(\mathbf v_k\)\(N\)-dimensional unit vector assigned to outcome \(k\), \(\mathbf v_0=(1,0,\dots,0)\)
  • \((n,k,d)\)\(n\) parties, \(k\) settings per party, \(d\) outcomes
\(d\)\(\mathbf v_0\)\(\mathbf v_1\)
2\((1)\)\((-1)\)
3\((1,0)\)\(\bigl(-\tfrac12,\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\bigr)\)
4\((1,0,0)\)\(\bigl(-\tfrac13,\tfrac{2\sqrt2}{3},0\bigr)\)

For \(d=3\), \(N=2\) and \(\mathbf v_0,\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2\) are the vertices of an equilateral triangle; for \(d=2\) only \(\pm1\) remain, as in CHSH.

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
CHSH has only ±1 outcomes. When each device gives d outcomes, give each outcome an arrow so that the arrows balance, then add them up.

The multi-component correlation function

n-party multi-component correlation function
\[ \vec Q_{i\cdots jk}=\sum_{m,\dots,n,s=0}^{N}\mathbf v_m\cdots\mathbf v_n\mathbf v_s\,P(A_i=m,\dots,B_j=n,C_k=s)=\sum_{t=0}^{N}\mathbf v_t\,P(A_i+\dots+B_j+C_k=t) \]
  • \(\mathbf v_a\circ\mathbf v_b=\mathbf v_{\bmod(a+b,d)}\)the "product" of vectors is addition of outcomes modulo \(d\)
  • \(P(A_i+\cdots=t)\)coincidence probability: the sum of outcomes \(\equiv t\pmod d\)
  • \(\vec Q=(Q^{(0)},\dots,Q^{(d-2)})\)\(d-1\) components

Eq. (3), Meng et al., arXiv:2311.03681, 2023, p. 2. For \(d=2\) there is a single component and the correlation reduces to the CHSH \(\langle A_iB_j\rangle\).

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Instead of one correlation number, we gather the probabilities "outcomes add up to t" into a vector that keeps all the information about the outcomes.

The full-correlated Bell function

Full-correlated Bell function and Bell quantity
\[ \begin{aligned} I_{n,d}&=\sum_{i,\dots,k=0,1}\omega_{i\cdots k}\,A_i\cdots C_k=\sum\omega_{i\cdots k}\vec Q_{i\cdots k},\\ B_{n,d}&=\sum\omega^{\prime}_{i\cdots k}Q^{(0)}_{i\cdots k}=\sum_{i\cdots k}\sum_{r=0}^{N}\omega_{j,r}P(A_i+\dots+C_k=r)\le L \end{aligned} \]
  • \(\omega\)real coefficients, linear combinations of \(\mathbf v_0,\dots,\mathbf v_N\)
  • \(L\)upper bound under local hidden variables (LHV)
  • \(Q^{(0)}\)first component of \(\vec Q\)

Eq. (4)-(5), Meng et al., arXiv:2311.03681, 2023, p. 2. The quantity \(B_{n,d}\) is the coincidence-probability form, generalising the \((3,2,3)\) inequality of reference [11] in the paper.

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
A Bell function is a weighted sum of all correlations. With clever weights the sum exceeds the LHV bound when the system is truly entangled.

Representing projectors with the observable

Projectors from the observable (prime d)
\[ X=\sum_{i=0}^{N}\mathbf v_i\Pi_i,\qquad v_0\Pi_i=\frac1d\bigl(v_0 I+v_{-i}X+v_{-2i}X^2+\cdots+v_{-Ni}X^N\bigr),\qquad X^j=\sum_t v_{\bmod(jt,d)}\Pi_t \]
  • \(\Pi_i\)rank-one projector of an ONB (orthonormal basis)
  • \(v_0=1\)first component of \(\mathbf v_0\)
  • \(d\ \text{prime}\)needed so that the matrix of \(v_{\bmod(jt,d)}\) is invertible

Appendix A, Meng et al., arXiv:2311.03681, 2023, pp. 3 and 9. The proof solves system \((A1)\) with a Fourier-Vandermonde matrix over \(\mathbb Z_d\).

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
The observable X and its powers "package" all projectors. This lets us split an n-party Bell function into two (n−1)-party ones.

The iteration formula for (n, 2, d)

Theorem 1: the iteration formula (prime d)
\[ \mathcal I_{n,d}=\frac1d\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal I^{0,0}_{n-1,d}&\mathcal I^{0,1}_{n-1,d}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}-\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}(d-k)\mathbf v_k&-\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}k\mathbf v_k\\[2pt]-\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}k\mathbf v_k&\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}k\mathbf v_k\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}C_0\\C_1\end{pmatrix} \]
  • \(\mathcal I^{0,0},\mathcal I^{0,1}\)two full-correlated \((n-1,2,d)\) Bell functions
  • \(C_0,C_1\)the two observables of the \(n\)-th party

Eq. (11), Meng et al., arXiv:2311.03681, 2023, pp. 3-4. It follows from \(\mathcal I_{n,d}=\sum_{k_0,k_1}\mathcal I^{k_0,k_1}_{n-1,d}\Pi_{k_0}\Pi^{\prime}_{k_1}\) (Eq. 10). For \(d=2\): \(\sum_k kv_k=\sum_k(d-k)v_k=v_1\).

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
To build an n-party Bell function you only need two (n−1)-party ones and a fixed 2×2 matrix. That is the "iteration": add one party at a time.

The iteration formula recovers MABK

The case d = 2: MABK
\[ \mathcal I_{n,2}=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal I^{0,0}_{n-1,2}&\mathcal I^{0,1}_{n-1,2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}-v_1&-v_1\\-v_1&v_1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}C_0\\C_1\end{pmatrix},\qquad \mathcal I^{0,1}_{n-1,2}=\mathcal I^{0,0}_{n-1,2}\ \text{with}\ X_0\leftrightarrow X_1 \]
  • \(v_1=-1\)for \(d=2\) outcomes are just \(\pm1\)
  • \(X_0\leftrightarrow X_1\)swap the two settings of every existing party

Critical visibility as the robustness measure

Critical visibility
\[ \rho=v|\Psi\rangle\langle\Psi|+(1-v)\frac{I}{d^n},\qquad v>v_c=\frac{L-NL_I}{NL_{|\Psi\rangle}-NL_I} \]
  • \(|\Psi\rangle=\tfrac1{\sqrt d}\sum_j|j\rangle^{\otimes n}\)the \(n\)-qudit GHZ state
  • \(NL_{|\Psi\rangle},NL_I\)quantum maximum of \(B_{n,d}\) on \(|\Psi\rangle\) and on the white noise \(I/d^n\)
  • \(v_c\)the smaller, the more noise-robust

p. 4, Meng et al., arXiv:2311.03681, 2023. Two equivalent inequalities share the same \(v_c\). This is the only yardstick the paper uses to compare Bell functions.

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Mix the clean state with white noise. The more noise you can add while the inequality is still violated, the more "robust" the inequality.

Unbiased symmetric (d × 2)-port beam splitters

Coincidence probability with symmetric beam splitters
\[ P(A_i+\cdots+C_k=r)=\frac{d+2}{d^2}\sum_{t=0}^{d-1}\sum_{s=t+1}^{d-1}\cos\!\Bigl(\phi^{(A_i)}_s-\phi^{(A_i)}_t+\cdots+\phi^{(C_k)}_s-\phi^{(C_k)}_t+\frac{2(s-t)r\pi}{d}\Bigr) \]
  • \(\vec\phi=(\phi_0,\dots,\phi_{d-1})\)phase vector defining each measurement (local macroscopic parameter)
  • \(U_{QFT}U(\vec\phi)\)measurement = phase, then quantum Fourier transform, then computational-basis readout

Eq. (13), Meng et al., arXiv:2311.03681, 2023, p. 4. Valid for the maximally entangled GHZ state. (Summation range as printed in the text extraction; check Eq. 13 of the original before reproducing numbers.)

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Each measurement is built from phase shifts and a Fourier transform. Just tuning the phases reaches the largest Bell violation.

The limit for classical and coherent states

Classical fields cannot violate
\[ E(\theta_1,\theta_2)=N^{-1}\!\int P(\boldsymbol\alpha)\,\bigl(|\gamma_+|^2-|\gamma_-|^2\bigr)\bigl(|\delta_+|^2-|\delta_-|^2\bigr)d^2\alpha,\qquad P(\boldsymbol\alpha)\ge0\Rightarrow |B|\le2 \]
  • \(P(\boldsymbol\alpha)\)Glauber–Sudarshan P-representation of the field state
  • \(\gamma_\pm,\delta_\pm\)c-number amplitudes after the polarisation rotation (13.20)
Trap: "strong correlation" is not yet a Bell violation

Every state with a positive, normalisable P-representation (coherent states included) obeys \(|B|\le2\). To violate it you need a nonclassical state such as an entangled photon pair, Eq. (13.21)-(13.25), Walls & Milburn, Quantum Optics, 2nd ed., Springer 2008, p. 252.

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
A laser beam (a coherent state) gives strong correlations yet stays "classical". That is also why the relay paper can use coherent states without entanglement.

The Clauser–Horne inequality and the Ou–Mandel experiment

The Clauser–Horne inequality
\[ S=P(\theta_1,\theta_2)-P(\theta_1,\theta_2^{\prime})+P(\theta_1^{\prime},\theta_2)+P(\theta_1^{\prime},\theta_2^{\prime})-P(\theta_1,-)-P(-,\theta_2)\le0,\qquad S=\tfrac{\eta}{4}(\sqrt2-1)>0 \]
  • \(P(\theta_1,\theta_2)=\tfrac\eta4\sin^2(\theta_1+\theta_2)\)coincidence probability, 50/50 beam splitter (Eq. 13.38)
  • \(\eta\)transmission of the attenuator in the idler beam
📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
When a polariser has only one output, the ±1 trick of CHSH is unavailable. Clauser–Horne works directly with coincidence probabilities, so it still applies.

A strong parametric source loses the violation

The limit \(\kappa\gg1\)
\[ S\propto F(\psi)-2G(\psi)\le 0,\qquad S\approx\tfrac14\kappa^2F(\psi)\ (\kappa\ll1) \]
  • \(\kappa\)parametric amplification strength of the source
Trap: a stronger source does not mean a larger violation

For \(\kappa\gg1\) the correlation coincides with the classical prediction (13.44), so there is no violation. Only \(\kappa\ll1\) (near a single photon pair) violates, with the maximum at \(\psi=\pi/8\).

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Many photons at once blur the correlation until it looks like a classical wave. See the curves: deep for small κ, shallow for large κ.

Equivalence classes and inequivalent functions

Equivalence classes and two functions with the same v_c
\[ A_iB_j\mapsto A_{\bar i}B_{\bar j},\qquad A_iB_j\mapsto v_{a_i}A_i\,v_{b_j}B_j,\qquad |[\mathcal I_{2,3}]|=54,\ |[\mathcal I_{3,3}(1)]|=648,\ |[\mathcal I_{3,5}(1)]|=1250 \]
  • \(\bar0=1,\bar1=0\)swap the two settings (observable symmetry)
  • \(v_{a}\)relabel the outcomes (outcome symmetry)
Trap: equal v_c does not imply equivalence

\(\mathcal I_{5,3}(1)\) contains the coefficient \(-4\mathbf v_1\), \(\mathcal I_{5,3}(2)\) does not. Symmetries only turn \(\omega\) into \(\omega v_1\) or \(\omega v_2\), so the two are inequivalent although both have \(v_c\approx0.488756\) (Eq. 17-18).

Equivalence follows Masanes (ref. [24] of the paper): party swaps, setting swaps, outcome relabelling. For \(n=5\) the equivalence check exceeds the authors’ computing capacity.

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Equivalent functions differ only by renaming. Two with equal robustness that no renaming can turn into each other are genuinely different inequalities.

Trap: do not call it "most robust" in general

The paper only says the functions found by the iteration formula are as robust as the known inequalities for the configurations searched. It proves no general optimality; the authors state they "have not found more robust" ones.

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
The formula is a guided search tool, not a theorem about the best possible. Use it within its scope: prime d, two settings per party.

Eq. (13.5d) in the printed Walls & Milburn, Quantum Optics, 2nd ed., Springer 2008 (p. 249) shows \(b_-\sin\theta_2\); an orthogonal rotation needs \(b_-\cos\theta_2\), as in (13.5b). Self-check: \(|c_+|^2+|c_-|^2\) must equal \(|a_+|^2+|a_-|^2\), which (13.22) relies on.

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners
Relay Charlie guesses the bit and re-sends a fresh state to Bob. The long path is cut in two, so each hop has milder fading.

Information leakage at the relay

Guessing success of the relay
\[ P_{guess}=1-P_e,\qquad P_e^{(ideal)}=\tfrac12\Bigl(1-\sqrt{1-e^{-4\gamma^2}}\Bigr) \]

📖 Plain-language explanation for beginners

Detailed notes

Example 1 (optics): CHSH at 22.5°

  1. Pick \(a=0^\circ,a'=45^\circ,b=22.5^\circ,b'=67.5^\circ\).
  2. Each \(E=\cos2\Delta\): \(\cos45^\circ=0.7071\) for three pairs, \(\cos135^\circ=-0.7071\) for \((a,b')\).
  3. \(S=0.7071-(-0.7071)+0.7071+0.7071=2.828\).

Example 2 (optical communications): minimum visibility

  1. A photon link has measured visibility \(v=0.75\) (illustration).
  2. \(S=2\sqrt2\cdot0.75=2.121\).
  3. \(2.121>2\), so the link still certifies a Bell violation; at \(v=0.65\), \(S=1.838<2\) and it does not.

Example 3 (finance-style, assumed): n-party noise threshold

  1. (Assumed) A distributed sensor network has up to 35% white noise (\(v=0.65\)).
  2. MABK: \(v_c=2^{-(n-1)/2}\). For \(n=2\): \(0.707>0.65\), not detected.
  3. For \(n=3\): \(v_c=0.5<0.65\), detected. Adding a party rescues the test.

Example 4 (probability): Helstrom bound for two coherent states

  1. \(\bar n=\gamma^2=0.5\): \(e^{-4\bar n}=e^{-2}=0.1353\).
  2. \(\sqrt{1-0.1353}=0.9299\).
  3. \(P_e^{\min}=\tfrac12(1-0.9299)=0.0351\).

Example 5 (networking): two-hop relay

  1. Both hops have \(P_{e,1}=P_{e,2}=0.01\) (illustration).
  2. \(P_e=0.01\cdot0.99+0.99\cdot0.01=0.0198\).
  3. Nearly double a single hop; the relay helps only if each hop is much better than the direct link.

Example 6 (biomedical-style, assumed): QKD over fibre

  1. (Assumed) A hospital uses B92 with two nonorthogonal states, \(|\langle\psi_0|\psi_1\rangle|^2=0.1\).
  2. \(P_e^{\min}=\tfrac12(1-\sqrt{0.9})=0.0257\).
  3. Eve cannot discriminate the two states perfectly, so every eavesdrop leaves a statistical trace (Walls–Milburn, 16.2).

Theoretical and historical background

Bell (1964) showed that any local hidden-variable theory yields bounded correlations; CHSH (1969) put that bound into a lab-testable form; Aspect (1982) measured \(B=2.697\pm0.015\), far above 2. Later work extended it to many parties (Mermin, Ardehali, Belinskiĭ–Klyshko) and many outcomes (CGLMP). Meng et al. (2023) contribute an iteration formula that builds an \(n\)-party Bell function from \((n-1)\)-party ones.

Exam traps

  • Mixing \(|B|\le2\) (CHSH, \(\pm1\) outcomes) with \(B\le1\) of the coincidence form; the two agree only up to a constant.
  • Forgetting that a smaller \(v_c\) means more robust.
  • Using the iteration formula for non-prime \(d\).
  • Treating coherent states as entangled: they satisfy CHSH.

Plain words, then technique

Plainly: a Bell test asks whether two distant players "cheat" with a shared script. Technique: compute a weighted sum of correlations and compare with the LHV bound. Table: \(v_c\) per \((n,d)\). Calculation: \(v_c=(L-NL_I)/(NL_\Psi-NL_I)\).
